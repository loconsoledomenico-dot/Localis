CAP. 2 - VICO DEL GARGANO: THE VILLAGE OF CITRUS AND ALLEYWAYS
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

Vico del Gargano is considered one of the most beautiful villages in Italy.

It's not one of those made-up rankings. Anyone who goes there understands why.

The medieval center is intact - alleyways so narrow two people brush shoulders, arches linking houses overhead, stone steps that seem to go on forever. The village is built on rock, literally. Some of its foundations are bare bedrock.

What strikes you in the early morning is the quiet. Not the quiet of emptiness - there are people here, shutters opening, the smell of coffee drifting out of doorways. It's the quiet of a place that moves at its own pace and has no interest in hurrying for anyone.

Cats sleep on windowsills. Elderly women carry shopping bags uphill without breaking stride. The stones underfoot are worn smooth by centuries of footsteps.

Around the village are the citrus groves. Vico is famous for its late-ripening mandarins - a local variety that matures in February, when the rest of Italy has no more fresh citrus. In winter you smell the village before you see it. Like Rodi, but denser. Sweeter. A fragrance that arrives around the last curve of the road, before the rooftops even come into view.

There's also the Foresta Umbra a few kilometers away. Vico is the gateway village for those entering the park from the north. In summer tourists rush through on their way to the sea. That's a mistake. Vico deserves a whole morning - getting lost in the alleyways, stopping for coffee, buying mandarins if they're in season, sitting still long enough to feel the rhythm of the place.

CAP. 3 - ISCHITELLA AND LAKE VARANO
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

Ischitella sits above Lake Varano.

Six hundred meters above sea level, the village looks down on the lake from above. It's a position you don't forget. From certain points you see the lake in front of you, the Adriatic beyond, and the Gargano stretching eastward. All of it at once, in one glance.

I've stood there on cold January mornings with frost on the ground and the lake still below, silver-grey in the early light. The air is clear in a way it never is in summer. Distances that seem impossible become visible.

Lake Varano is the largest coastal lake in Italy. Sixteen kilometers long, separated from the sea by a strip of sand. Like Lesina, but bigger and wilder. Less known. Less visited. More authentic.

The eels from Varano are famous throughout Puglia. The lake has produced them for centuries - the brackish water creates perfect conditions. Fishermen use techniques passed down from generation to generation, lowering wicker traps into places they know by heart. That kind of knowledge lives in the hands and the eyes, not in books.

Ischitella itself is small - fewer than two thousand inhabitants. But it has a square with a view worth the journey. Elderly men sit outside in the evenings and watch the lake change color at sunset. They've done it all their lives. They don't think it's extraordinary. It is.

Let me tell you something you won't find in any travel guide.

On State Road 693 dei Laghi, near Cagnano Varano, there's a service station. Station Petrolgas. Around kilometer thirty-two. Fifteen minutes from Rodi.

Stop there.

Not for the petrol. For the bar.

There's a raised terrace overlooking the lake, and on clear days you can see the Diomedee on the horizon. The Tremiti, as they're called today.

It's the most scenic service station I know. The locals know it. People stop for coffee, look at the lake, and drive on. One of those places that don't appear on maps but survive in the memory of those who live here.

CAP. 4 - THE EUCALYPTUS GROVE: A STORY FEW KNOW
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

If you drive along the coast between Foce Varano and Capoiale, at some point you'll see a grove unlike anything else.

Tall trees, bark peeling in pale strips, long leaves that smell of menthol. They are not native trees. They are eucalyptus. Right by the sea, on the edge of the coast, a distinctive grove you don't expect to find.

Its story is a story of mistakes and remedies.

In the nineteen-fifties and sixties, the Italian state launched a reforestation program for the Gargano. The original forests had been decimated over the centuries - felled for timber, burned to make way for pasture. The vegetation cover had to be rebuilt.

Eucalyptus was chosen. It grows fast, adapts to poor soils, withstands drought. It seemed like the perfect solution.

The problem is that eucalyptus is not a Mediterranean tree. It doesn't create real ecosystems. Very few animal species use it. The soil beneath it is almost sterile. It doesn't hold water the way native oaks do.

Walk under eucalyptus in the Gargano and notice how thin the life feels. Fewer birds. Less movement in the undergrowth. A landscape with a shape, but not much life inside it.

And yet that grove has become something of its own - recognizable, characteristic, something the locals know from a distance. Today the Gargano National Park is working to reintroduce native species little by little. It will take decades. In this work, the right direction is almost always the slow one.

CAP. 5 - THE FIRE: JULY 24, 2007
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

There's a date that no one who works on this promontory ever forgets.

July 24, 2007.

It was a Tuesday. The wind was blowing hard from the west. It was the height of July - the Gargano was full of tourists, the campsites packed, the beaches crowded.

That day a fire devastated Peschici.

Not a fire far away in the woods. A fire that reached the village itself. The campsites. The houses.

Gas cylinders exploded. Smoke was visible for tens of kilometers. Three hundred people were injured - tourists and residents together. Hectares of scrubland were destroyed.

Three people died.

Domenico De Nittis, sixty-two years old, a merchant. Carmela Fasanella, eighty-one years old. Romano Fasanella, seventy-one years old. The Fasanella siblings were found burned in their car.

Many people fled into the water. Local fishermen went out with their boats to rescue them. Emergency crews worked through the night. The whole coastline became a line of fear, smoke, sirens, and people trying to understand which direction was still safe.

I was on duty that day. Fifteen hours in the field.

When you come home after something like that, you can't sleep. You close your eyes and you still see the fire. You hear the sound it makes - a low continuous roar, like something breathing too fast and too close to your face.

And then there's the smell. Burned resin. Burned grass. Burned plastic. It stays on your clothes. It stays in your hair. It stays in the back of your throat.

Years later, when the wind comes from the west and the humidity drops, my body remembers before my mind does. The shoulders tighten first. Then the eyes begin to scan the horizon without being told.

CAP. 6 - AFTER THE FIRE: WHAT REMAINS AND WHAT RETURNS
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

Fire does a strange thing to a landscape.

It burns everything - scrub, trees, nests, burrows. It leaves behind black ground, bare and silent, that looks dead forever.

It's not dead forever.

Three weeks after the blaze, if it rains, the first grasses begin to appear. Tiny at first. Fragile. So small you could miss them if you weren't looking for them.

After a year, the first shrubs are already there. After five years, the low forest is back. After twenty years, you may barely see any trace of it unless you know where to look.

Part of what makes this possible is the soil itself. Mediterranean soil has memory. Seeds of native plants can lie dormant for years, waiting for the right moment. Fire clears the competition and lets light reach the ground. Some species even need heat to germinate - cistus, rock rose, seeds whose hard coat opens only under stress.

It seems brutal, and it is. But regeneration is built into this landscape.

What is not built into it is repetition. Fires that return every five or ten years don't give the forest time to grow between them. Then you don't get recovery. You get exhaustion.

That's why a park ranger's work after a fire doesn't end with the extinguishing. It begins with the extinguishing. You have to manage the recovery - planting oaks and holm oaks, protecting seedlings from wild boar, watching what returns on its own and what needs help.

It's years of work. Quiet work. Work most people never see.

But when I walk back into forests burned in two thousand seven and find oaks two meters tall where there was ash, I understand why I do it. Not because the fire is forgotten. It isn't. Because life came back anyway, and someone had to stay long enough to help it.

CAP. 7 - ARSONISTS: THE TRUTH NOBODY WANTS TO SAY
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

I need to tell you something people don't like to say out loud.

Most of the fires on the Gargano are not accidental.

It's not a spark. It's not a cigarette butt. It's not the heat.

It's hands.

People who set fires deliberately.

The reasons vary. Property speculation - a burned forest can more easily become buildable land. Family rivalries. Economic interests I don't understand and don't want to understand.

The Archbishop of Manfredonia, after the fire in Vieste in 2024, said something I agree with word for word: these fires are criminal acts committed against the humanity of today and tomorrow.

We park rangers know. We know from the way they start, from where they ignite, from multiple outbreaks happening at the same time. A forest doesn't catch fire in three different spots at the exact same moment by accident.

We know it. The investigators know it. And yet convictions are rare, sentences light. That gap - between what is known and what is proven - is one of the hardest things to live with in this work.

This is the heaviest part of the job. Not the fire itself. Knowing that someone chose it.

CAP. 8 - VARANO: THE LAKE IN WINTER
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

In summer Lake Varano is almost ignored by tourists.

Everyone goes to the sea. The lake stays there, still and quiet, with fishermen heading out at dawn and returning by mid-morning.

In winter the lake becomes extraordinary.

Migratory birds stop here. Herons, cormorants, wild ducks, spoonbills. At certain times of year you see flamingos - not as many as at the Saline di Margherita di Savoia, but they're here. Pink, motionless in shallow water, their thin legs looking barely strong enough to hold them.

The winter light over the lake is unlike any other light I know. It comes in low and flat and turns everything metallic - silver, pewter, the sudden flash of white from a bird taking off.

The reeds at the edge of the water are pale gold. When the wind moves through them they sound like very quiet applause.

I've spent whole mornings there with binoculars and a thermos of coffee, not moving, just watching. Those mornings cost nothing and give back more than I know how to measure.

The lake in November, with low mist over the water and reeds bending in the wind, is one of the most beautiful landscapes I know. Not the Gargano of postcards. The real Gargano. The one that exists after the tourists are gone.

CAP. 9 - WHAT TO DO: THE INLAND VILLAGES
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

First: don't stop at the sea alone.

Give at least half a day to the villages. Vico del Gargano is twenty minutes from the coast. Ischitella is half an hour. From Peschici you're up there in ten minutes. This is not a demanding excursion. It's a different decision.

For Vico: go early in the morning or late in the afternoon. At noon in summer the center is hot and crowded. In the evening the village comes alive - people come outside, bars set tables in the street, and you eat well for very little.

For Lake Varano: go by bike or on foot along the embankment. There are marked routes that follow the lake for kilometers. It's flat, shaded, and you'll barely meet anyone.

For the forests: stay on marked trails. Don't light fires. Ever. Not even a barbecue. Not even a cigarette. In summer the risk is real and the damage lasts twenty years.

One more thing, and I say it to everyone who passes through my section of the park: take your rubbish with you. All of it. Plastic bags, bottle caps, fishing line, everything. The forest doesn't digest plastic. The animals don't either.

The people who leave that behind don't see what comes next. I do.

CAP. 10 - THE PARK'S ANIMALS: THE ONES THAT CAME BACK
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

In the Gargano National Park there are animals that weren't here twenty years ago.

The wolf has returned.

There had been no wolves in Puglia for centuries. Then, slowly, they began to come down from the Apennine ridge toward the south. Now they're here. Not many - a few packs, a few solitary individuals. I see them on camera traps. I almost never see them in person.

Once, early in the morning, I was walking near the upper edge of the forest and found fresh tracks in the mud. I followed them for a few hundred meters and then stopped, because the tracks stopped too - right at the edge of a clearing.

Whatever had made them had stood there for a moment and then turned back into the trees.

I stood still and listened. Nothing moved. But something had been there minutes before. That feeling - of an animal that close, just out of sight - is one I don't forget.

There are also fallow deer - the Gargano fallow deer, a native subspecies, darker than the continental one. Wild boar, numerous and less shy than you'd expect. The black woodpecker in the beech forests. The rock partridge on the high ledges.

The park is not a zoo. These animals do not appear on demand. You see them if you're patient, if you stay still, if you go at the right hours - dawn and dusk. Or if you work here long enough to learn how to read signs most people walk past.

CAP. 11 - THE TRAILS: HOW YOU WALK HERE
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

The Gargano National Park has a network of trails.

Not all of them are marked the same way. Some are excellent - well maintained, easy to follow. Others are tracks that only locals know properly. My advice is simple: take a paper map. Don't rely on GPS alone. In some forests the signal doesn't reach.

The routes around Vico del Gargano and Ischitella are among the best. You walk through olive groves and citrus orchards, with Lake Varano on one side and the sea on the other. In spring the citrus blossom scents the entire valley.

The best time to walk here is October. The heat has gone. The tourists have gone. The late-afternoon light turns golden and horizontal. The ground softens after summer and the earth begins to smell alive again.

I've led groups on these trails in October and watched people who arrived tense from cities begin to slow down, visibly, step by step. By the end of a three-hour walk they move differently. The land does something to you if you let it.

For the trails in the Foresta Umbra - the stretch between Vieste and Monte Sant'Angelo - you need real trekking boots. The ground is roots and earth. After rain it gets slippery. In summer the forest stays cool even when outside the heat is punishing.

CAP. 12 - CARPINO AND FOLK MUSIC
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

There's a village near here called Carpino.

Most tourists don't know it. About a thousand inhabitants, perched on the hillside, looking out over Lake Varano. From the outside it looks like any other inland Gargano village.

But Carpino has a musical tradition centuries old. The tammurriata. The battente guitar. The fishermen's songs from the lake. Music that smells of sea and mountain at the same time, like the place where it was born.

The battente guitar is different from a classical guitar. Four double strings. Played hard. It drives rhythm more than melody. When you hear it in a small square at night, bouncing off stone walls, it gets into your chest before it gets into your ears.

Every year in August the Carpino Folk Festival takes place. Musicians come from all over Puglia, from other parts of Italy, sometimes from abroad. It isn't a tourist performance. It's the real thing - kept alive by people who study this music and live inside it.

I've been four times. At night, in the historic center, with music striking the stone and returning from every side, it becomes one of those experiences no travel guide can really prepare you for.

At the end of the day, when everything is finally quiet, I sit on a rock above the lake. I watch the water. I wait. Sometimes a fallow deer comes down to drink. Those five minutes repay the rest.

CAP. 14 - FIRE AND EARTH: WHAT GROWS AFTER A BLAZE
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

People often ask me: after a fire, how long before the forest comes back?

The answer depends on the fire.

A fast fire, one that moves across the surface - the roots survive. They sprout again in spring. In three years the forest is recognizable.

A deep fire, one that scorches the earth - that's different. The one in Peschici in two thousand seven was deep. The soil burned. The roots burned. What grew back afterward is not the same thing that was there before.

A landscape can return in shape before it returns in substance. People often mistake the two.

Now, almost twenty years later, in some areas the Mediterranean scrub has re-established itself - lentisk, rosemary, rock rose. You see green again. You smell rosemary again when the sun warms the slope. But the old pines, the ones that had been there for decades, are gone.

That is the part people need to understand. Green is not the same thing as restored.

It takes a century to make a forest. To destroy it, five hours are enough.

What you plant matters too. After major fires, we try to use local seed sources - plants grown from seeds collected in the same area. Genetic material already adapted to this soil and this climate. It takes more time. It costs more money. But it gives the land a better chance of becoming itself again.

That's the logic. Slow. Expensive. Unseen by most people. And right.

CAP. 15 - LAKE VARANO: THE STORY OF AN ENCLOSED WATER
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

Lake Varano was not always a lake.

Or rather - it was always water, but not always the same water. Long ago it was a bay open to the sea. Then sediments from the Candelaro and other streams gradually built the sandbar that separates it from the Adriatic. Grain by grain, over millennia, the bay became a lagoon and the lagoon became a lake.

Today the only connection with the sea is through the inlets to the east. The water is brackish - neither fully fresh nor fully salty. Perfect for certain fish. Perfect for eels. Perfect for birds that need a transitional environment.

What amazes me is that the lake is still largely unknown. Sixteen kilometers of water, with an entire ecosystem of birds, fish, plants, and light, and most people drive past it on their way somewhere else.

Partly that's because it doesn't photograph the way the sea does. No cliffs. No theatrical turquoise. Just water, sky, reeds, wind. You have to slow down to see it.

In winter there are flamingos here too - a few, not like at Margherita. But they're here. I see them from the embankment in the low light of January, and every time it catches me off guard.

CAP. 16 - THE VILLAGES: WHY THEY'RE STILL INHABITED
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

A question I'm asked often: how come these villages haven't emptied out?

Partly they have. Vico del Gargano had more inhabitants thirty years ago. So did Ischitella. Young people leave for Foggia, for Bari, out of Puglia altogether. That part is true.

But the villages hold on. They don't disappear.

Because there are still families tied to the land - to the olive groves, the citrus orchards, the rhythms of work here. Because slow tourism has begun to arrive too - people who want the Gargano beyond the beach, who rent a house for a week and come looking for quiet rather than entertainment.

And because some young people come back. Not all. Not many. But some. Someone leaves at twenty, spends fifteen years in Milan or abroad, and then returns to open an agriturismo or a workshop or a small business. They come back with skills, ideas, a different way of looking at the place.

I think what they find here is scale. Things are still the right size for a human being. Distances are walkable. Community is knowable. The seasons are visible.

My work brings visitors who might never come without the park. Guided walks, birdwatching, trekking. Not huge numbers. But people who sleep in the villages, eat in the villages, spend in the villages.

A drop. But drops count.

CAP. 17 - TIME HERE: SLOW AND SEASONAL
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

Time here moves with a rhythm the city has forgotten.

In spring you prune, plant vegetable gardens, prepare equipment. In summer you endure the heat and take a holiday if you're lucky. In autumn you harvest olives and prepare for winter. In winter you mend what broke, stay close to home, and wait for the light to return.

It's an old clock. It doesn't suit everyone. It requires a capacity to be still, to wait, to repeat. But for those who have that capacity, the rhythm is a form of sanity.

And the seasons make themselves known very clearly here. In March the almond trees blossom white against the hillside before anything else has properly woken up. In May the wildflowers come up along the trail edges. In September the air smells of figs and warm stone. In December the light turns red-gold by late afternoon and the forest goes quiet in a way that feels deliberate.

Nothing is subtle about the seasons here. Each month announces itself.

I work inside that rhythm every day. The park follows the seasons. The animals follow the seasons. I can't force any of it. I have to wait.

And in that patience I've learned more than I learned any other way.

CAP. 18 - CLOSING: STEFANO SAYS GOODBYE
voice: alFofuDn3cOwyoz1i44T

Thirty years on this promontory.

I've watched forests burn that my father once took me to see as a child. I've seen trees come back where I thought it was impossible. I've seen villages empty in winter and fill again in summer. I've seen tourists arrive in ever greater numbers.

The Gargano holds up under all this because it's large. Large and varied. It isn't just beaches. It's forests, lakes, villages, hills, work, memory.

Those who see it only from the sea see one tenth of it.

I've spent thirty years trying to understand this place and I still find things that surprise me. A fresh wolf track in a section of forest I've patrolled for years. A plant I don't recognize on a trail I've walked a hundred times. An evening light over the lake that makes the whole landscape look new again.

That is the gift of staying somewhere long enough. The place becomes more generous, not less.

I ask just one thing. When you're here, treat this place as if it belongs to someone. Because it does. It belongs to me, to my son, to the children of the fishermen and rangers who work here all year long.

It also belongs to the trees that need fifty years to become shade again, and to the birds that only return if a place stays quiet long enough.

Take it with you in your memory. But leave it intact.

This promontory has other faces.

The sea at Vieste. The islands on the horizon you first saw from the lake. The bell towers of Monte Sant'Angelo. The trabucchi coast at sunset. Each one has someone who can tell it. Someone born there. Someone who worked there. Someone who stayed.

The Gargano doesn't end here.
