GARGANO — VIESTE AND PUGNOCHIUSO
Voice: Rossella, 54 years old, owner of a historic beach club in Vieste, third generation

=====
CAP. 1 — INTRO: MY GRANDFATHER'S BEACH CLUB
voice: valerio

My grandfather opened this beach club in 1963.

He had just bought a deck chair and an umbrella. That was it. The beach was free, the water was clean, and tourists were almost nowhere to be found.

My name is Rossella. Fifty-four years old, third generation. After my grandfather came my father, after my father came me. I raised two kids here on this beach — literally. I used to push the stroller right down to the water.

I remember the first summer I ran things alone — my father had hurt his back and I was twenty-six, no idea what I was doing, just trying not to drown. A fisherman from down the harbour came by in the afternoon and said: your grandfather would be proud of how you set out the umbrellas. Straight lines, just like him. That was enough for me. I straightened up and kept going.

I have watched this coastline change in sixty years more than it changed in the six hundred before that. I saw the Germans arrive, then the Dutch, then Italians from the north, then the Russians, then everyone all at once. I have seen beach clubs built where there was only rock. I have seen roads cut through where there were footpaths. I have watched hotels grow up on headlands where we used to go and pick wild fennel.

But the sea has stayed the same. The same emerald green. The same white rock. The same crystal-clear water you can see straight through to the bottom. The same smell — salt, iodine, sun on limestone — that hits you the moment you step out of the car after a long journey. Some mornings I walk down to the shore before sunrise and just stand there, barefoot on the wet sand, and I cannot believe I get to live here.

I will tell you about Vieste the way someone who lives here year-round knows it — not just in summer.

=====
CAP. 2 — VIESTE: THE WHITE CITY
voice: valerio

Vieste is the easternmost city in mainland Puglia.

The old town sits on a promontory of white limestone that drops straight into the sea. On one side, Pizzomunno beach — the big one, all sand, the one you see in every photo. On the other, Castello beach — smaller, more sheltered, the one the locals prefer. I prefer it too. Less wind, calmer water, and in the early morning the light on the castle wall is a deep gold you could stare at for an hour.

The Pizzomunno is the white rock monolith standing in front of the main beach. Sixteen metres out of the water. Legend has it he was a young fisherman turned to stone — the sirens took him away and separated him from his beloved Cristalda. Every hundred years they meet for one night. Then he turns back to stone.

My grandmother used to tell me this story when I was little. Then you grow up and you understand it is just a legend. But every time I see the Pizzomunno at dawn, when the light has not quite arrived yet, I think of it again. There is something about a column of white stone rising from the sea that resists being simply geological. You look at it and your mind starts telling stories.

The old town of Vieste — il vicoletto, as we call it — is a maze of white alleyways, staircases, archways, terraces overlooking the sea. Every corner opens onto something unexpected — a small square with a single lemon tree, a doorway with hand-painted tiles, a cat sleeping on warm limestone. The Norman cathedral at the top, twelfth century, built over a pagan temple. And the Chianca Amara — the bitter stone — where in 1554 the Turks under Dragut beheaded five thousand inhabitants. Not a legend. History.

=====
CAP. 3 — THE BEACH: WHAT THE PHOTOS DON'T SHOW
voice: valerio

Photos of Vieste always show the same thing — a beach packed with umbrellas, green water, the Pizzomunno in the background.

It is beautiful. But it is not everything.

The coast around Vieste has thirty kilometres of beaches and rocky coves. Some reachable by car, others only on foot or by boat. The most beautiful beaches have no names on maps — only the locals know them, and people with boats.

Sciale delle Femmine, Cala Punta Lunga, Baia dei Mergoli — little white-rock coves where the water is so clear you can see the bottom three metres down. No beach umbrellas. No bars. Just the rock, the sea, and you. The silence is different there — not empty but full, the way only a place with no roads nearby can be. You hear the water moving against the rock, a gull overhead, your own breathing.

I go to the beach every morning at half past six, before I open up the club. Thirty minutes for myself, in the still-cool water, with nobody around. In July the water is already warm enough to stay in for twenty minutes. In September it is warmer still — the Adriatic holds the summer heat long after the air has cooled. I float on my back and look up at the sky and I think: this is what my grandfather wanted. Not the business. This. That is my Vieste. Not the August one.

=====
CAP. 4 — PUGNOCHIUSO: THE SECRET RESORT
voice: valerio

Pugnochiuso is not a village.

It is a bay. A private bay, tucked between two white-rock headlands, ten kilometres south of Vieste.

In the 1960s a Milanese entrepreneur — Ovidio Presutti — bought this bay and built a holiday village here. Not just any village — a luxury resort with hotels, bungalows, restaurants, a marina. In the middle of nowhere. On a coastline that had no roads.

In the seventies and eighties, Pugnochiuso was where the important people went. Industrialists, politicians, actors. Milanese families who came south in summer and wanted the Gargano sea without giving up comfort. My father used to say you could always tell the Pugnochiuso guests at the harbour: they were the ones with the linen shirts pressed perfectly, even in August heat.

My grandfather used to deliver fresh fish there every morning. He would go up with a crate, hand it over to the chef at the main hotel, then come back. He told me it was a different world up there — people in white linen, children with sandals, aperitivo on the terrace. He came back smelling of pine resin and kitchen herbs. I asked him once what it was like inside. He said: like a film set. Beautiful but not quite real. He preferred our beach.

The resort still exists today, changed, less exclusive than it once was. But the bay is still there — one of the finest in the Gargano, sheltered, with water that in the early morning hours turns a deep, intense emerald green. I have taken my own children there by boat. They jumped from the rocks without asking permission, the way children do. My grandfather would have liked that.

=====
CAP. 5 — THE SEA AT VIESTE: HOW TO REALLY SEE IT
voice: valerio

Let me tell you how the people who know see the sea at Vieste.

By boat.

Not the motorboat with tourists lined up in rows. A small rubber dinghy, or a wooden gozzo, with a boatman who knows the coast. You set out early in the morning, follow the coast south, and go inside the caves.

Grotta Campana, Grotta delle Viole, Grotta del Diavolo — sea caves open to the water where light enters from below the surface and turns the inside electric blue. You cannot reach them on foot. Only from the sea.

Then you anchor in a cove. Swim. Eat something. Head back. That is the perfect day on the Gargano — and most tourists never do it. They stay on the beach all day and miss the best part. They go home and tell their friends they loved Vieste, and they did — but they saw about half of it.

The boatmen at the harbour know all of this. Find one who goes out early, before nine, before the wind picks up from the south. Ask him to take you as far as Pugnochiuso and back, stopping where the mood takes him. Do not give him a schedule. Let him show you.

CAP. 6 — VIESTE IN SUMMER AND WINTER
voice: valerio

Vieste gets two hundred and fifty thousand visitors in August.

For a town of fourteen thousand people.

You can imagine what that means. Every cafe has a queue. Every restaurant has a waiting list. Every car park is full from nine in the morning. The sea is just as beautiful — but it is not a relaxing experience. By midday in August the Pizzomunno beach looks like a patchwork of umbrellas and sunburned backs as far as you can see.

I love Vieste in April. When the weather is still unpredictable, sometimes raining, sometimes blazing sunshine. The sea is still cold but already green. The bars in the old town are open and there are only locals. You can find a seat. You can actually talk to people. The fishermen are at the harbour in the afternoon, mending nets, willing to spend twenty minutes explaining exactly where they caught what and why. That conversation does not happen in August — there is no time and no silence.

In winter, Vieste goes quiet. Many shops close. The seafront is empty. The north wind stirs the sea into something genuinely dramatic — grey-green waves against white limestone, spray carried all the way up to the cathedral square. And the town goes back to being what it always was — a place of fishermen and traders living their own lives regardless of tourists. I love those months. The bakeries are warm, the coffee tastes better, and I finally get to have a full conversation with my neighbours.

=====
CAP. 7 — WHAT TO EAT IN VIESTE
voice: valerio

Fish. Always fish.

Vieste has a fish market early in the morning — the fishermen unload at the harbour and sell direct. If you are up by seven, you will find quality fish you would not get at any restaurant for that price. Bring a cool bag if you are staying in an apartment. Or just buy something to eat raw on the spot, the way the locals do — a handful of clams, still cold from the sea, opened right there on the harbour wall with a small knife.

Grilled octopus is the beach club dish. Fresh octopus, slow-grilled, with olive oil, lemon, parsley. Nothing else. Simple, perfect. The octopus needs to be fresh and it needs time on the grill — not rushed, not overcooked. When I do it right, and I usually do, you can smell it from three rows of umbrellas away.

Sea urchins — if you are here between July and September. You will find them at a few spots near the harbour, cracked open on the spot, with a squeeze of lemon. Or on pasta — spaghetti ai ricci is the dish I order when I want to eat really well. The flavour is concentrated ocean. There is nothing else quite like it. Do not order it if you are unsure — it is intense, mineral, not sweet. But if you like it, you will dream about it afterwards.

For dessert: the mostacciolo from Vieste. A hard, spiced biscuit with honey and almonds. Made at Christmas and found all year round. You dip it in wine. It is the most ancient thing you can eat here. My grandmother kept a tin of them under the counter of the beach club all summer. Every afternoon, around five, she would hand one to any child who was still hanging around. Most of those children are my age now. Some of them still come.

CAP. 8 — THE PIZZOMUNNO: THE LEGEND
voice: valerio

The Pizzomunno is the symbol of Vieste.

A monolith of white limestone, twenty-five metres tall, standing in the middle of the beach like a sentinel. When you see it for the first time you understand why people reach for their cameras. It seems impossible — a column of white stone rising from the sea.

The legend says Pizzomunno was a young fisherman. He was in love with a girl called Cristalda — the most beautiful in the village. The sirens of the sea fell for him and tried to take him away. He refused. Out of spite, the sirens dragged Cristalda into the deep. Pizzomunno watched her disappear and turned to stone. Still there. Waiting for her.

My grandfather used to tell me this story when I was little. He would say: you see, Rossella, whoever waits too long ends up turning to rock. I still do not know if that was a warning or a promise.

What I do know is that on certain evenings, when the sun drops behind the headland and the light turns gold-pink on the stone, and the last swimmers of the day are coming out of the water, and the beach is going quiet — the Pizzomunno does not look like a rock formation. It looks like someone waiting. Very patiently. Very still. And you feel slightly guilty walking past without acknowledging it.

=====
CAP. 9 — THE TRABUCCHI: FISHING ON THE CLIFF EDGE
voice: valerio

Between Vieste and Peschici the coastline changes.

No more wide, long beaches — it becomes sheer rock faces, white cliffs, the sea twenty, thirty metres below. And on those rocks, clinging like spiders, are the trabucchi.

The trabucco is an ancient fishing machine. A wooden platform jutting out over the cliff, with a system of beams and ropes that lower a net into the sea. No boat required. No strong wind needed. You wait for the fish to pass and drop the net. It is a form of fishing that requires patience above all else. You sit, you watch the water, you wait. For someone who grew up on this coast, that kind of waiting is not empty — it is full of information.

The trabucchi along this stretch of coast are the most spectacular in the Gargano. At sunset, with orange light on the water, they look like silhouettes from another century. The whole structure is in wood — beams, pulleys, netting, platforms worn smooth by sea wind — and yet it holds. It has held for generations. Some have been turned into restaurants — you eat right on top of them, with the sea below your feet and the net still going up every now and then. The fish on those menus was caught within sight of your table. That is not marketing. That is just how it works.

=====
CAP. 10 — THE OLD TOWN: THE WHITE VILLAGE
voice: valerio

The old town of Vieste is white.

Not as a metaphor — genuinely white. The houses are whitewashed with lime, the streets are narrow, the alleyways interlock in a way that defies any description in words. You have to walk it to understand it. Even then, you will get lost the first time. Probably the second time too. That is not a problem — that is the point.

The old town stretches along a promontory reaching into the sea. On one side the harbour. On the other the beach with the Pizzomunno. At the top, the Cathedral — Romanesque, built in the eleventh century, with a crypt where the columns are Roman. Someone took them from Sipontum, the ancient Siponto. Stones get recycled in Puglia. History here is not something in a museum — it is underfoot, overhead, built into walls you lean against in the afternoon shade.

There is also the Chianca Amara — the bitter stone. A stone slab in the old town where enemies are said to have been executed. Until just a few decades ago, an effigy of the Turk was burned here every year. The memory of certain centuries does not fade easily. I think that is right. We should not pretend it was not hard. It was. And this town survived.

=====
CAP. 11 — THE HARBOUR: FISHERMEN AND TREMITI
voice: valerio

The harbour in Vieste has two sides.

On one side the fishing boats — blue and white hulls, nets drying in the sun, stacked plastic crates, the smell of early morning sea. Vieste's fishermen go out at night and come back at dawn. The restaurants in the old town buy their fish from them. I buy from them too — always have, always will. You can tell the difference in the grilled octopus. Trust me on this.

On the other side, the ferries to the Tremiti Islands. In summer the crossings are frequent — the trip takes about an hour, depending on the boat. My grandfather used to go by motorboat, forty minutes. He said you could spot the islands from far away — three green smudges on the blue. On a clear day in July you can see them from the beach with the naked eye, low shapes on the horizon that could be clouds except they do not move.

If you want to take the ferry to the Tremiti, leave from Vieste — it is the closest departure point. Book in advance in summer. And go early in the morning: the sea is flat and the light on the islands is something else. Come back in the late afternoon, when the sun is behind you all the way. The crossing home is often the best part.

=====
CAP. 12 — THE COVES: ONLY BY BOAT
voice: valerio

The finest things about Vieste cannot be reached on foot.

Along the coast between Vieste and the Gargano there are coves the road never touches. Small rocky inlets, clear water, not a sunshade in sight. You can only get there by boat.

In summer, rubber dinghy excursions leave from the harbour. They follow the coastline, enter the sea caves, stop in the coves. There are some — I will not name them, because the name does not change anything — where the water is such an intense green it looks unreal. You do not believe it until you are inside it. I have taken people there who have seen tropical water, Caribbean water, and they still stopped talking when we anchored in one of those coves. The colour is not like anything else. Something about the white limestone floor, the depth of the water, the angle of the morning light.

If you have your own boat you can move freely. Renting a small dinghy without a licence is possible — ask at the harbour about the allowed engine size. It is the best way to see this coast. The way my grandfather used to do it, and the way I still do. Pack bread, cheese, something to drink, and do not plan the route. Follow the coast and stop where the water looks inviting. It always does.

=====
CAP. 13 — VIESTE AND TOURISM: A HUNDRED YEARS OF CHANGE
voice: valerio

My grandfather used to tell me about when Vieste had three thousand inhabitants.

Fishermen, farmers, a few traders. Tourism did not exist. The roads were not paved all the way to Foggia. People were born here, lived here, died here. The same surnames in the same houses for generations. My grandfather knew every family in the village by name, and their grandparents, and their history.

Then in the sixties and seventies tourism arrived. First campsites. Then hotels. Then holiday homes. Today in August Vieste has over a hundred thousand people — thirty-five times the permanent population.

The change brought work. It brought roads, services, infrastructure. It also brought traffic, high prices, the feeling that in summer the town is no longer yours. I watch young people leave Vieste in September — not just the tourists, but some of the locals too — and I understand it. The cost of living here has gone up while the winter work has not. That is a real problem, not a nostalgic one.

My grandfather used to say: before, we ate the fish we caught. Now we sell it to tourists. Is that better or worse? I still do not know how to answer him. I think it is both. Most things are.

=====
CAP. 14 — THE TURKS IN VIESTE: 1554
voice: valerio

In 1554 an Ottoman fleet led by Admiral Dragut arrived off the coast of Vieste.

What happened next is still alive in this city's memory today. Thousands of inhabitants were taken prisoner and carried off as slaves. The town was ransacked. The churches, the houses, the harbour — all of it devastated.

In the old town there is a stone slab called the Chianca Amara — the bitter stone. They say that is where prisoners were counted before being loaded onto ships. I do not know how historically accurate that is — legends tend to encrust themselves over the facts with time. But the stone is there. And the memory is there. And every child in Vieste grows up knowing that the sea, which gives everything, can also take everything away in a single morning.

My grandfather used to tell me: Rossella, this city is afraid of the sea. It lives off it, eats off it, makes money off it — but it does not trust it. The sea takes things away. This city knows that. I think he was right. There is a particular kind of respect here for what lies beyond the harbour wall. Not fear, exactly. Awareness.

=====
CAP. 15 — THE WHITE CLIFFS: SEEN FROM THE SEA
voice: valerio

The coast at Vieste looks completely different from the water than from the shore.

The white cliffs — limestone, a hundred metres of vertical rock — can only be taken in fully from the water. From below, on a small boat, they look enormous. You understand why nobody ever built there. You understand the scale of this place. From the road above you see a wall of green forest ending at the edge. From the sea you see the other side: raw white stone going straight down into deep blue water. Nothing in between.

The sea caves open up at the base of the cliffs. Some you can enter by boat — low and slow, timing it with the waves. Inside the echo changes. The water changes colour. The sound of the engine closes in around you, then the engine cuts and there is only water and rock and the sound of waves amplified into something almost ceremonial.

My grandfather used to take me out on the boat from when I was five. I was not scared — I did not know what fear of the sea was. He taught me to read the water — the way the colour changes over sand compared to rock, the way a dark patch means depth, the way a ripple on flat water can mean a current underneath. Now that I know what I know, I respect it even more. But I keep going.

=====
CAP. 16 — OLIVE OIL: THE GARGANO INLAND
voice: valerio

Everyone comes to Vieste for the sea. Almost nobody goes inland.

A few kilometres from the coast — north, into the hills — there are olive groves. Old trees, twisted trunks, some of them centuries old. You drive past them on the road and they look like they have been there since before the road was built, because they have. Gargano olive oil is recognised as a quality product — native varieties, late harvest, fruity and slightly bitter flavour. Not the sweet kind. This is oil that tastes like it came from somewhere with wind.

In November, when the beach season is over, the olive harvest begins. The inland villages come alive. Families head to the groves. The oil mills open. The smell of fresh oil drifts through the streets — it is one of the best smells I know, heavy and green and slightly sharp, nothing like the bottled version. If you are here in autumn and you see an oil mill with its doors open, go in and ask if you can watch. They will almost always say yes. Buy a bottle of the new oil before you leave. Pour it on bread the same evening. That is the Gargano I grew up with, before the beach season started.

This is the Gargano you do not see in summer. The one that existed for a thousand years before tourism — farming, fishing, hard work. I belong to both worlds. I was born by the sea but I know the olive trees too.

CAP. 17 — HOW TO GET THERE AND GET AROUND
voice: valerio

Vieste is not easy to reach — and that is exactly why it is so beautiful.

By car: from Foggia, follow the state road through the Gargano — about an hour and a half. The road climbs, descends, winds — it is not a fast road. But the views along the way are worth every minute of driving. At a certain point you come around a bend and the sea appears below you, far down, impossibly blue against the white rock, and for a moment you forget you are driving.

By bus: there are connections from Foggia. Not very frequent. Extra services are added in summer. Check the timetable before you set off. The journey takes longer than by car — the buses stop in every village along the way. But in the off-season it is a fine way to see how the inland towns live: a man with a basket getting on at one stop, a schoolgirl at the next, a priest at the one after that.

By ferry: from Manfredonia, from Bari, from Ancona. In summer there are direct links with many cities on the Adriatic coast. It is the most beautiful way to arrive — watching Vieste come closer from the sea, with the white cliffs growing on the horizon, is already an experience in itself. You arrive already in the right mood.

Once you are there: park outside the centre and walk. The old town is pedestrianised. In summer the traffic is blocked anyway. On foot is better. On foot is the only way to find the good things.

=====
CAP. 18 — VIESTE AT NIGHT: SUMMER AFTER SIX
voice: valerio

In summer in Vieste, after six in the evening, the town changes.

The beach tourists have showered. They are getting dressed. Between seven and ten the main street fills up — ice cream, tables outside, children running around. The air drops a few degrees — not much, but you feel it. The quality of the light changes too: the low sun turns the white limestone walls amber, and for an hour before dark the whole old town looks like it is lit from inside.

The restaurants in the old town open at lunch but fill up in the evening. You do not always need a reservation, but in August at the weekend it is worth booking. Eat outside when you can — the white alleyways look different at night. The lamps are soft, the walls close in gently, and the sound of plates and voices bounces around in a way that makes everything feel festive without being loud.

My grandfather never liked summer. Too many people, he would say. But even he, in the evenings, would sit outside with a glass of white wine and watch the lively seafront. He would never admit it — but he was happy. I know because I know him. I knew him. I have been sitting in that same chair in the evenings for thirty years, watching the same street, and I understand now what he was looking at. Not the tourists. The light on the water at the end of the promenade, going dark by degrees, as it does every single evening, exactly the same and never quite the same.

CAP. 19 — THE SEA CAVES: WHAT YOU SEE INSIDE
voice: valerio

The sea caves around Vieste are among the finest in the Gargano.

You cannot see them from the shore. You cannot see them from the road. They open at the base of the cliffs, below the waterline in some cases, and you can only reach them in a low boat moving slowly. The entrance can be narrow — a metre of clearance above the water, sometimes less. You duck your head and the cliff is right above you and then suddenly you are inside.

Inside the caves the water changes colour. Light enters from below — reflected off the sandy floor — and paints the walls in sea green, turquoise, deep blue. Cut the engine, wait for your eyes to adjust. Then look up at the walls above you. The rock is not smooth — it is folded, rippled, layered like sediment, which is what it is: millions of years of seabed compressed and lifted and carved by water. What you see inside those caves is the inside of the Gargano, which is to say the inside of time.

I was brought there by dinghy as a child. My grandfather always cut the engine inside the biggest cave. He would say: listen. The echo of water lapping against the walls was like music. I have never heard anything like it anywhere else. I still go. I still cut the engine in the same place. Some things you keep doing because they are right, not because anyone told you to.

=====
CAP. 20 — PUGNOCHIUSO: THE STORY OF THE RESORT
voice: valerio

Pugnochiuso is a place you do not expect.

A bay between white cliffs, reachable only by sea or through a private resort that has been there since the 1960s. Club Mediterranee opened here in 1962 — one of the first in Italy. They chose this spot because it was isolated, because the water was extraordinary, because back then nobody had yet discovered the Gargano.

The resort has changed hands but still exists. The private beach, the bungalows in the woods, the sports facilities. It is not what it once was — nothing is. But the bay is the same. The cliffs are the same. The water is the same. There is a particular quality to the light there in the morning — the sun comes over the eastern headland and hits the water at an angle that turns it a colour that does not have a good name in English or in Italian. We just call it the colour of Pugnochiuso.

Those who are not staying at the resort can reach it by boat. From out at sea, you see it set into the rocks like a gem — the white bungalows barely visible in the pine forest above the beach, the wooden dock below. You understand why someone in the 1960s stopped and said: here. I would have said the same thing.

=====
CAP. 21 — CLOSING: ROSSELLA SAYS GOODBYE
voice: valerio

Sixty years of this beach club. First my grandfather, then my father, then me.

I have watched everything change — the tourists, the prices, the fashions, the nationalities. Once they were all Italian. Then the Germans came. Then everyone. I have watched the umbrellas go from canvas to synthetic and back again. I have watched the sunbed change shape four times. I have watched my grandfather's one deck chair become a hundred, and the price of espresso go from nothing to something that would have made him put his cup down in disbelief.

One thing has not changed.

Early in the morning, before the club opens, before the customers arrive — there is an hour when the beach is mine. The water is flat, the sun is still low, there is no sound but the waves. The sand is cold and firm under my feet where the tide has smoothed it. The Pizzomunno is there at the end of the beach, white and still. The old town is still sleeping on its rock above.

In that hour I understand why my grandfather opened a beach club here with a deck chair and an umbrella. Not to make money. To be in this place. To have a reason to be in this place every single morning for the rest of his life.

Enjoy Vieste. Get up early. The sea in the early morning is something else entirely.

This headland has other faces.

The trabucchi at sunset towards Peschici, the archangel's grotto carved into the rock, the islands you can see from the water on clear days, the quiet villages above Lake Varano. Each one has someone who tells its story. Someone who never left.

The Gargano does not end here.
