MANFREDONIA, SIPONTO AND THE SALT FLATS OF MARGHERITA DI SAVOIA
Voice: Antonello, 54 years old, salt quality control officer, from Manfredonia, works at the Margherita di Savoia salt flats

CAP. 1 — INTRO: THE SIX O'CLOCK SHIFT
voice: valerio

The six o'clock shift is the best.

In summer the sun rises over the sea, behind the basins, and the water turns pink. It's not an optical illusion. It's the light filtering through dissolved salt, through the microclimate of the salt flats, through the millions of microorganisms that colour the most concentrated basins.

My name is Antonello. Fifty-four years old. I'm from Manfredonia — born there, live there, always go back there. But I work here, at Margherita di Savoia, at the salt flats. Thirty years.

My job is called salt quality control. I measure water density, sodium chloride concentration, the purity of the final product. Every day. Every season. Since I was twenty-four.

I've seen these basins at every possible hour. In the pink dawn of July, in the December fog, in the orange sunset when the flamingos all rise together and the sky fills with wings.

I carry a small kit with me — a refractometer, a few test strips, a notebook that's always damp from the humidity. The refractometer is how I read the water: you take a sample, you hold it up to the light, you read the scale. After thirty years the reading is almost automatic. My hands know before my eyes do.

This morning the air smells of iodine and dry grass. The embankment road is still dark on one side and already bright gold on the other where the sun catches it. I walk this stretch every single working day and I still notice things I haven't noticed before — a different colour in a basin, a new cluster of flamingos in a spot they don't normally use, a crust of salt forming at an unusual angle.

Some people ask me how I haven't gone mad doing the same job for thirty years. I tell them: there is no same job. The salt is never the same. The water is never the same. The birds change, the light changes, the seasons change. I am the only fixed point out here, and even I'm different depending on the morning.

If you want to understand this stretch of Puglia — the salt, the gulf, the ancient cities behind you — come with me.

CAP. 2 — THE SALT FLATS: HOW SALT WORKS
voice: valerio

The Margherita di Savoia salt flats are the largest in Italy.

Four thousand hectares of basins, channels, embankments. A hydraulic system built over centuries, refined, never fully automated — because salt is not just another industrial product. Salt is alive.

The process is simple in theory. Seawater enters the evaporation basins — the first ones, called mother waters. Sun and wind evaporate the water. Salt concentration increases. The water moves from one basin to the next, getting denser, getting more coloured.

When the concentration is right — and I'm the one who says so, with my instruments and thirty years of trained eye — the water enters the crystallisation basins. There the salt settles to the bottom. A white layer forms, compact, crystalline.

Harvesting happens in summer, between July and September. Machines scraping the bottom, mountains of white salt piling up along the banks. From a distance they look like snow. In August, with forty degrees of heat, it feels like a joke nature is playing.

What people don't realise is how much wind matters. More than the sun, sometimes. A dry tramontana blowing for three days in a row can do more for evaporation than a week of sunshine with still air. I always check the weather forecast before writing my concentration predictions. Wind is my invisible colleague.

The basins are not all the same, either. The first ones — the ones closest to the sea intake — have relatively dilute water, pale blue-green in colour, barely saltier than the Adriatic. As the water moves inward, as the kilometres of channels do their slow work, the colour deepens. The intermediate basins go orange-brown. The final crystallisation basins, in peak summer, are sometimes almost red — a deep brick-red from the halophilic archaea, the microorganisms that thrive at extreme salt concentrations.

I remember the first time I saw one of those red basins. I was a young technician, still learning the job. I thought something had gone wrong — some contamination, some accident. My supervisor laughed. He said: that's the colour of perfection. That's the water doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

He was right. The red means the concentration is at its maximum. The salt is ready. That colour is the signal I've been working towards all season.

CAP. 3 — THE PINK FLAMINGOS
voice: valerio

The flamingos are here almost all year.

Since 1992 they've settled in permanently. In summer they're most numerous — from June to September the basins fill with pink. But I see them in winter too, in smaller groups, in the central basins. Since 1997 they've been breeding here. They've become the symbol of Margherita.

The greater flamingo — Phoenicopterus roseus — is not a tropical bird. It lives in the Mediterranean. It breeds in Sardinia, in the Camargue in France, in Spain. And here it found a place to stay.

Their pink colour comes from what they eat — microscopic algae and crustaceans rich in carotenoids. In the salt flat basins there's plenty of all that. A flamingo that doesn't eat these foods loses its colour. It turns white.

Early in the morning, when I arrive at work, sometimes I stop for five minutes on the roadside. I watch them. Thirty years and I never get tired. There's something impossible about a flamingo at dawn. It shouldn't exist, and yet there it is.

The sound they make is something people don't expect. Not elegant — almost honking, nasal, loud. A big group of flamingos sounds like a distant crowd at a football stadium. The first time visitors hear it they always look around confused. Then they look at the birds and realise.

What I find most extraordinary is watching them feed. They lower their heads upside-down into the water — the beak working in reverse, filtering the brine through a specialised plate structure inside the jaw. Evolution spent a very long time designing that beak. It's perfectly matched to this environment, to this exact salinity range. If the water is too dilute or too concentrated, the food organisms aren't there and the flamingos move on. They are, in a way, the most accurate indicator of water quality I have. When the flamingos are feeding actively in a basin, I already know the concentration is within a good range before I take out my refractometer.

I've started to think of them as colleagues.

CAP. 4 — MARGHERITA DI SAVOIA: THE CITY OF SALT
voice: valerio

Margherita di Savoia is a city built on salt.

That's not a metaphor. The town was born as a settlement for salt workers — the salinari. The oldest neighbourhoods, the low houses near the embankment, the warehouses: everything exists because of salt.

Salt from Margherita had enormous value for centuries. Before industrial food preservation, salt was the only way to preserve fish, meat, vegetables. Whoever controlled salt controlled food. Whoever controlled food controlled everything.

Even today, the thermal baths of Margherita di Savoia are famous. The mother waters of the salt flats — those high-concentration basins I use for my quality checks — are used for therapeutic treatments. Dermatitis, psoriasis, joint pain. People come from all over Italy.

Walking through the old part of town, you see it in the architecture. The low ceilings of the workers' houses — designed for a life spent mostly outdoors, in the heat, on the embankments. The wide doorways of the old warehouses where salt was stored before shipment. The little chapels the salinari built with their own hands on their days off. None of it is grand. All of it is functional. A town that never pretended to be something other than what it was.

I've always respected that. Pretending is exhausting. This town never bothered.

The salinari were a specific class of people — skilled workers, not just labourers. Reading the water, managing the channels, deciding when to move water from basin to basin, when to close a gate, when to open another — these were skilled decisions. Mistakes had consequences. Too early and the salt doesn't form properly. Too late and the crystal breaks down in summer rain. It's not so different from what I do now with instruments. The difference is that the old salinari did it all by eye and experience. Thirty years in and I'm still learning things they probably knew in their first decade.

CAP. 5 — THE GULF OF MANFREDONIA
voice: valerio

When I look north from these basins, I see the Gargano.

The promontory rises from the water like a sleeping animal. The shape is unmistakable — the dark mass of the forest, the white cliffs facing the sea, the high silhouette of Monte Sant'Angelo.

The Gulf of Manfredonia is a wide gulf, open to the east onto the Adriatic. To the north the Gargano closes it like a wall. To the south the coast opens towards Barletta, towards Bari, towards the rest of Puglia.

This gulf has always been a thoroughfare. Ships coming from the East passed through here. Pilgrims heading to Monte Sant'Angelo landed here. The Saracens came to raid here. And Manfredi of Swabia, eight hundred years ago, decided this was the place for a city.

I know this gulf in all its moods. In June when the water is flat and pale turquoise and the horizon disappears in the haze, you can't see where the sea ends and the sky begins. In November when a scirocco comes in from the southeast, the gulf turns dark and the waves roll long and heavy all the way from Albania. In January, if the tramontana blows hard enough, the surface of the shallow basins nearest the sea can get a thin skim of ice near the edges — just a centimetre, just for a morning, but enough to remind you that this coast is not as mild as people imagine.

The light over the gulf changes hour by hour in ways that are almost ridiculous. I've been trying for years to describe it to people and I always fail. You have to be here at different times. The same water that looks white at noon looks copper at five in the afternoon and deep violet at dusk. The Gargano in the background changes colour with it — from dark green to grey to almost black. It's like living inside a painting that someone keeps repainting over the top of itself.

I never get tired of it. Thirty years of the same view and I never get tired.

CAP. 6 — MANFREDONIA: THE FOUNDER'S CITY
voice: valerio

Manfredonia is a young city, by Puglia's standards.

It was founded in 1256 by Manfredi of Swabia — son of Emperor Frederick II, King of Sicily, one of the most powerful men of his time. Manfredi chose this point on the gulf because it was sheltered, because the harbour was natural, because from here he could control the Adriatic routes.

Before Manfredonia there was Siponto. But Siponto was disappearing — earthquakes, malaria, a silted-up harbour. Manfredi moved Siponto's inhabitants to the new city that bore his name. A city built from scratch, by design, with straight streets and a castle at the centre.

I was born there. And every time I walk through the historic centre, I still see the original layout — the medieval street axes, the alleys that slope down to the harbour, the castle looking out to sea. A city that was planned, not grown by chance.

What strikes me is how confident that act was. You don't found a city unless you believe in the future. You don't plan streets and walls and a castle unless you expect people to still be living there centuries later. Manfredi was right. The city is still there. He isn't — he died in battle five years after founding it — but the city is.

My neighbourhood is in the old centre, two blocks from the cathedral. The streets are narrow enough that in summer the shade saves you. The stones underfoot are worn smooth. On Thursday mornings there's a market in the main piazza — vegetables, fish, a few clothes stalls. It's been happening every Thursday for longer than anyone can remember. The city has had good centuries and terrible centuries, but the Thursday market continues regardless. I find that reassuring.

CAP. 7 — THE SWABIAN-ANGEVIN CASTLE
voice: valerio

The castle of Manfredonia is one of the finest in Puglia.

Built by Manfredi in the thirteenth century, expanded by the Angevins in the fourteenth, modified by the Aragonese and then the Spanish. Every ruler left their mark — a taller tower, a deeper moat, a different bastion.

Today the castle houses the National Museum of the Gargano. Worth visiting for the Daunian stelae alone — carved stone slabs left by a pre-Roman people who lived on the Gargano three thousand years ago. Human figures, animals, hunting scenes. A writing that isn't writing yet — images that tell stories.

From the walkway along the walls you can see the entire gulf. The Gargano to the north. The salt flats to the south. The sea ahead. Manfredi knew how to pick his spots.

I've been inside that castle dozens of times. As a child my parents brought me there on Sunday afternoons — it was free, it was cool inside in summer, and the stones had stories to tell. I didn't always understand the stories then, but I understood that there was something serious happening in those walls. That people had lived and fought and made decisions inside them that changed this part of the world.

The Daunian stelae are the ones that stay with you. They're not beautiful in the conventional sense — they're rough, schematic, the figures barely human. But they have a directness that classical sculpture often lacks. These were people marking their existence. This person lived. This person mattered. The carvings were grave markers, found in necropolises across the Gargano area. Three thousand years ago, someone in this landscape thought: I need to leave a trace. And the trace is still here.

I think about that sometimes when I'm doing my quality reports at the salt flats. Numbers, measurements, data — my own kind of trace. Less dramatic, certainly. But not nothing.

CAP. 8 — SIPONTO: THE ABANDONED CITY
voice: valerio

Two kilometres from Manfredonia, near the beach, is what remains of Siponto.

It's not much, at first glance. A square, a few columns, the basilica. But Siponto was one of the great cities of medieval Puglia — a trading port, a bishop's seat, the embarkation point for pilgrims heading to the shrine of Saint Michael at Monte Sant'Angelo.

Pilgrims from all over Europe landed here. French, German, English — all of them passed through Siponto before climbing to the Gargano. The city was full of inns, hospitals for the sick, churches.

Then came the earthquakes, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The coastline subsided slightly — imperceptible to someone living a single lifetime, enormous across centuries. The marshes advanced. Malaria arrived. Siponto emptied slowly, like a cup with a crack in it.

What I find remarkable is that there was no sudden catastrophe. No single day when Siponto died. It drained away, gradually, across generations. People leaving one by one, then in groups, then the last few who had nowhere else to go or simply couldn't bring themselves to abandon the place they were born. The city was still there — the streets, the churches, the harbour. Just emptier each year. Until one day it wasn't a city anymore, just a collection of buildings in a marsh.

That's a harder kind of ending than a dramatic destruction. A siege, a fire, a battle — those you can point to. The slow draining of a place is harder to see. You're always in the middle of it, never at the end. The people who were there probably didn't know they were living through the death of their city. They just noticed there were fewer neighbours than last year.

When I walk through what's left of Siponto, I try to imagine the noise it must have had. The port sounds — caulking, loading, argument. The pilgrims asking for directions in languages no one understood. The bells of seven churches at once. Now there's just the wind in the pines and the occasional car on the coast road. The silence is so complete it almost has a texture.

CAP. 9 — THE BASILICA OF SANTA MARIA DI SIPONTO
voice: valerio

The Basilica of Santa Maria di Siponto is still standing.

It is one of the most beautiful Romanesque churches in Puglia — perhaps in all of Italy. Built in the eleventh century, in white stone, with a gallery of blind arches running along the outer walls. Simple. Solid. Like the people who built it.

But the extraordinary thing is not the church above. It's what lies beneath.

Going down into the crypt you reach a paleo-Christian basilica — a church from the fifth or sixth century, built when Siponto was still a living and powerful city. The walls are ancient. The floor still has its original tiles, worn smooth by seventeen hundred years of footsteps.

The Romanesque builders of the eleventh century did not demolish the older church. They built on top of it. They preserved it, the way you preserve something too sacred to touch.

When I go down into that crypt, I always think of my work at the salt flats. Salt preserves. Stone preserves. Certain things resist time better than we think.

The light down there is different from the light above. The crypt is lit from narrow openings near the ceiling — in the afternoon the light comes in at a low angle and strikes the old floor tiles at a slant, and you can see every crack and worn edge. The tiles are orange-red, the colour of old terracotta everywhere in this part of the world. Some of them are loose. You can hear them move under your feet.

I always go down alone if I can. The crypt is not a large space — twenty people and it feels crowded. But alone, or with one other person, it has a quality I can only call weight. Not uncomfortable weight. The weight of something that has been standing a very long time and intends to keep standing. The thick walls hold the temperature constant regardless of the season — cool in summer, warmer than you'd expect in winter. The building is a climate-control system built from stone.

Seventeen centuries of footsteps before mine. I add mine and move on. That's all any of us do.

CAP. 10 — SALT AND TIME
voice: valerio

Salt has a quality that almost nothing else in the world has: it doesn't deteriorate.

Salt found in Egyptian tombs is still salt. The salt in the deepest mines is millions of years old and hasn't changed. It preserves, protects, stops time for everything it touches.

Thirty years of early morning shifts have given me plenty of time to think about these things. I walk along the basins with my instruments, measuring density and concentration, and I think.

I think that this coast — the gulf, the salt flats, Siponto buried beneath, Manfredonia founded on a dead city — is a place where time works differently. Not slower. More visible. The traces don't disappear. They settle, like salt on the bottom of the basins, layer by layer.

There's a technical aspect to this that most people don't know. When salt crystallises in the basins, it doesn't do so randomly. The crystals form in a specific structure — sodium and chloride ions arranging themselves in a cubic lattice, the same geometry every time, from the beginning of the earth to now. The crystalline structure of salt produced in these basins today is identical to the crystalline structure of salt produced here a thousand years ago, when the Normans were running the operation. The atoms know what to do. They don't need instructions.

I find this oddly comforting. There is something in the world that works the same way regardless of who is watching, regardless of who is in charge, regardless of the politics and catastrophes and centuries of human noise. The salt forms. The crystal is cubic. The flamingos come back.

My job, in the end, is just to measure it. To write the number down. To say: yes, this is what it is. The salt was doing this before I was born and it will do it after I'm gone. I'm just the person who takes notes while it happens.

That's a good enough job, I think. A good enough life.

CAP. 11 — WHAT TO DO AND HOW TO GET THERE
voice: valerio

For the salt flats: the Margherita di Savoia Nature Reserve organises guided visits.

There's a nature trail along the embankments — on foot or by bicycle — that lets you observe the flamingos up close. Peak season is June to September, when flamingos are present in large numbers. Bring binoculars. And shoes that can get dirty — the embankments aren't always dry.

For Manfredonia: the castle is open to the public and worth visiting for the Daunian stelae. The historic centre is small and walkable in an hour. The harbour has good fish restaurants — the gulf is still generous with its catch.

For Siponto: the basilica is a few minutes' drive from Manfredonia, heading south along the coast road. Don't miss the crypt. It's free. Take five minutes of silence down there. It's worth more than any explanation.

A few practical things I'd add from experience. If you're visiting the salt flats in summer, go in the morning — the light is better for the colours, the heat is more manageable, and the flamingos are more active. By midday the heat over the embankments is serious and the light bleaches out the colours you came to see. Bring water. There's no shade on the trails.

If you have a bicycle, use it. The embankment roads are flat and the distances aren't huge. You can cover the most interesting sections of the reserve in two to three hours by bike without rushing. On foot it's possible but the summer heat makes it harder.

For the castle in Manfredonia, go when it's less crowded if you can — midweek mornings in shoulder season. The stelae deserve time. Don't rush through them. There are dozens and each one is different. Give yourself at least an hour just for that room.

And if you're here on a Thursday morning, go to the market in the main piazza. Buy some cheese. Buy some vegetables. Find a bar on the square and drink an espresso standing up while watching the stalls. That's what this city actually is, outside the tourist circuit. Worth thirty minutes of your time.

CAP. 12 — THE TRANSHUMANCE: THE TRATTURI OF THE GARGANO
voice: valerio

Before the salt, before the flamingos, before all of it — there were the tratturi.

Not machines — tratturi.

The tratturi are ancient grassy roads walked for centuries by shepherds driving their flocks between the mountains of Abruzzo and the plains of the Tavoliere in Puglia. In autumn they came down towards the sea. In spring they went back up to the mountains. The transhumance — this seasonal movement of flocks — shaped this landscape for three thousand years.

The Royal Tratturo that passes through these areas is sixty metres wide — a grassy motorway. Today it's partly swallowed by cultivated fields, partly paved over. But the route is still there — if you know what to look for, you can see it.

Every time I cross the plain between Manfredonia and Foggia I think of the shepherds who made this journey on foot. With thousands of sheep. For weeks. The salt they carried with them was as precious as gold.

The salt connection is direct and literal. Salt was essential for preserving cheese, for curing the meat the flocks produced, for keeping the animals themselves healthy during the journey. The trade routes between the salt flats and the mountain shepherds were among the most important economic connections in this region for centuries. The salt went north. The wool and cheese came south. It was a closed system, self-sufficient, working for generations.

You can still find traces of the tratturo if you walk the right fields. The grass on the old route grows differently — the centuries of animal hooves compacted the soil in a specific way that still affects drainage and therefore what plants grow there. Botanists can map the old routes by looking at what grows where. The landscape carries memory in its vegetation, not just its stones.

When the transhumance ended — when trucks replaced flocks, when refrigeration replaced salt — something changed in this landscape that was more than economic. A rhythm that had structured life here for three thousand years simply stopped. The tratturi are still there, in fragments, but the movement is gone. The silence on them now is a different kind of silence from the silence on an ordinary road.

CAP. 13 — THE TURKS AND MANFREDONIA: A DIFFICULT HISTORY
voice: valerio

Manfredonia has had quiet centuries and terrible centuries.

In the seventeenth century the Turks arrived.

An Ottoman fleet attacked the city — looted it, partly burned it, took people away as slaves. It was common practice throughout the Mediterranean in those centuries — the southern Italian coasts were frequent targets. But Manfredonia was hit hard.

The city recovered. It rebuilt. It carried on. But the memory of that violence remained — in the watchtowers along the coast, in the churches rebuilt afterwards, in family stories passed down without anyone realising.

When I stop at the port of Manfredonia and look out to sea, I think about how many times this shore has witnessed things it would rather not have seen. The gulf looks calm. But it has a long memory.

The watchtowers are still there, some of them — stone cylinders on headlands along the coast, built to give warning of approaching ships. A guard at the top, a signal fire ready to light. The system connected tower to tower, all the way along the coast, so that a warning could travel faster than any ship. It was an early telegraph, built from fire and stone. Some of the towers are restored, some are ruins, some have been converted into private houses. But you can still read the logic of the system if you look at a map — each one placed where it could see the previous and the next one in the chain.

Living with that kind of threat — the sea as a source of danger as much as food and trade — must have shaped the people here in ways that are hard to fully understand from the outside. The sea-facing walls of the old buildings are thicker. The windows facing the sea are smaller. Architecture that has absorbed fear and made it structural.

My grandfather had a saying about the sea: beautiful in the morning, treacherous by noon, deadly at night. He was talking about the weather. But I've always thought it was also about history.

CAP. 14 — THE PORT OF MANFREDONIA: FISHING BOATS AND EARLY MORNINGS
voice: valerio

The port of Manfredonia wakes up before I do.

When I arrive at the salt flats at six, the fishing boats are already out. They leave at night — at two, at three. They come back in the morning with what they've caught: clams, squid, red mullet, prawns. The gulf of Manfredonia is still generous.

The fish from this gulf has a quality you can taste. I can't explain it in chemical terms — I feel it in the flavour. The squid here have nothing in common with the ones that arrive frozen from who knows where. And the clams — I'd recognise the clams from this gulf anywhere.

When I'm not on shift, on Sundays, I stop by the port. I buy fish directly from the fishermen. I take it all home. I cook. My wife says I always keep it too simple — just oil and lemon. But fresh fish doesn't need anything else.

The port has a particular smell at that hour — diesel, brine, the cold mineral smell of fish fresh from the water. Not unpleasant, if you're from here. If you're from here, that smell means food and work and the particular competence of people who know what they're doing. The fishermen move with an economy of motion that comes from doing the same physical work since they were teenagers. No wasted gesture. The nets are handled a certain way, the crates are stacked a certain way, the cleaning of the catch is done fast and clean.

I've spent enough early mornings at that port to know some of the fishermen by name. Not friends — I'm from the land side of this town's economy, not the sea side. But faces I recognise, people who give me a nod. We're both in the business of reading natural things and responding to them. They read the sea. I read the basins. Different instruments, same general approach.

My wife tolerates my Sunday fish buying because the results are good, not because she enjoys the early hour. By eight o'clock she has fish on the table that was in the water four hours earlier. That's a fact that city people find almost unbelievable. For us it's just Sunday.

CAP. 15 — THE BEACH OF SIPONTO: ARCHAEOLOGY AND SEA
voice: valerio

The beach at Siponto is long and open.

No luxury hotels on the seafront — a few beach clubs, plenty of free shoreline. In summer the locals come — people from Manfredonia, from Foggia, the odd passing tourist. It's not Vieste, not as crowded as the Gargano to the north.

From the beach you can see the basilica. It's a hundred metres from the waterline — between the car park and the pine wood. It's strange to swim in the sea with twelve hundred years of history a hundred metres away. But in Puglia you get used to that.

Under the sand of this beach, and in the surrounding pine forest, there are still traces of Siponto that no one has excavated yet. Every now and then something turns up — a mosaic fragment, a column, remnants of walls. The city is still there, buried, waiting.

The pine forest between the basilica and the beach is itself something worth time. It was planted in the twentieth century — part of a coastal reforestation programme — and now it's tall and shaded and has a particular dry-resin smell in summer that I associate with childhood. My parents brought me to this beach when I was small. The pines were already there, already tall. I used to think they were ancient. Later I found out they were less than a hundred years old. Sometimes things that feel ancient are just things that arrived before you did.

The free beach at Siponto is genuinely free — no ticket, no umbrella to rent if you don't want one. You can put a towel on the sand and stay all day. In July and August it's crowded with families. In September, when the children go back to school, you can have long stretches of it almost to yourself. September sea in this gulf is warm and clean and the light is softer than in the height of summer. If I have a week's holiday, I take it in September. Every year, the same choice. Every year, correct.

CAP. 16 — SALT AND MEDICINE: THE THERMAL BATHS
voice: valerio

The waters of the Margherita di Savoia salt flats are not only for making salt.

The mother waters — those very high-concentration basins, where the water is dense and heavy — are used for thermal treatments. The Margherita di Savoia thermal baths are open and accredited by the National Health Service. This isn't wellness tourism — it's medicine.

Dermatitis, psoriasis, eczema — the salt-iodine treatment is documented as effective. People come on medical prescription, not just as tourists. I sent my brother there — he had a chronic skin problem that nothing else would shift. After two courses of treatment he improved significantly. I don't know the exact chemistry. I know what I saw.

The same waters the flamingos work in heal people. This place is strange and wonderful.

From a technical standpoint, I understand part of why it works. The very high mineral concentration of the mother waters — sodium chloride, yes, but also magnesium, calcium, potassium, iodine and a range of trace elements — has specific effects on inflamed skin tissue. The osmotic pressure alone changes what happens at the skin surface. Add the iodine, which has known anti-inflammatory properties, and the thermal temperature, and you have a combination that conventional medicine has validated, not dismissed.

What I think about is the continuity. People have been using these waters for their healing properties for a very long time — long before anyone understood the chemistry. They knew from experience that sitting in this water made certain things better. The knowledge came before the explanation. That happens more often than scientists like to admit.

The thermal baths building is not glamorous. It's functional, clinical, practical — a place people go to get better, not to feel luxurious. That suits the character of Margherita. The town has never been in the business of performance. It's been in the business of salt and of getting things done.

CAP. 17 — THE GULF IN SUMMER: WHAT TO EAT
voice: valerio

The gulf of Manfredonia is generous.

The squid from the gulf are famous throughout Puglia. Small, tender, fried or grilled whole. In Manfredonia you'll find restaurants serving them just off the boat — that's not a marketing promise, it's the reality of an active working port.

The clams from the gulf are another story. The sandy seabed favours venus clams — the ones you open in a pan with garlic, oil and white wine. Simple, quick, perfect.

I eat at the port on Sundays — trattorias with no big signs, tables outside and fish from that morning. No fixed menu. They tell you what's in. You choose from that. It's the best way.

One thing worth knowing: in Manfredonia, the raw seafood tradition is strong. Oysters, sea urchins, raw clams — consumed standing up at the port market, with lemon, nothing else. Visitors sometimes hesitate. I understand the hesitation but I'd encourage you to try it. The freshness makes the difference. Raw seafood that's truly fresh tastes completely different from what most people have encountered elsewhere. Clean, almost sweet, with a mineral finish that's exactly what the sea smells like. The gulf in your mouth.

The bread here matters too. Pane di Altamura, from the town inland, is the regional staple — a sourdough with a thick crust and dense crumb that holds up to oil and fish juice without falling apart. The combination of this bread, good oil from local olives, and fresh seafood from the gulf is one of those things that seems too simple to justify the satisfaction it produces. But it does. Every time.

If you're in Manfredonia in August for the feast of the patron saint, the food in the streets is worth staying for. Fried things, sweet things, grilled things. The whole town eating together outdoors. Salt is everywhere — on the fried food, in the sea air, in the history of the place. You can't escape it. You wouldn't want to.

CAP. 18 — THE WINTER SEA: THE GULF WHEN IT'S EMPTY
voice: valerio

In summer the gulf of Manfredonia is full of boats.

In winter it's empty.

Fishing boats, the occasional cargo ship in the distance, nothing else. The sea turns grey-green, the waves long and regular. The Gargano in the background looks darker — the vegetation doesn't shine the way it does in summer. The sky is low.

I prefer the gulf in winter. Not because it's more beautiful — it's more beautiful in summer. But in winter it's mine. When I arrive at the salt flats at six, there's no one there. Me, the flamingos, the wind. Nothing else.

In those moments I understand why I never left. Not for lack of opportunity. For this. For the winter mornings on the empty gulf.

In winter the salt flats have a completely different atmosphere from summer. The basins are lower — the harvest is done, the crystallisation cycle is over, the water level drops and the salinity decreases as rain dilutes the system. The colours are muted. The flamingos that remain are in smaller groups, quieter, feeding steadily in the central basins where the concentration stays higher. The embankment road is often wet. The wind comes off the Adriatic with nothing to slow it down.

I do my rounds faster in winter — not because the work requires less attention, but because the cold makes stopping uncomfortable. My hands are colder on the instruments. The notebook pages get damp faster. By nine o'clock, when the winter sun has been up for a couple of hours, the light is still low and flat, coming in from the southeast at a shallow angle, and the water in the basins reflects it like pewter.

This is not the Puglia you see in the travel photographs. The travel photographs are always summer — turquoise water, pink flamingos, golden light. That's real too. But this grey winter version is equally real, and to me it's equally beautiful. Perhaps more so, because almost no one else is watching.

CAP. 19 — THE CHARACTER OF MANFREDONIA'S PEOPLE
voice: valerio

The people of Manfredonia have a reputation.

Direct. Sometimes too much.

It's not rudeness — it's a way of being in the world. No ceremony, no beating around the bush. If they say yes, it's yes. If they say no, it's no. There's nothing to interpret.

I come from those people. My father worked at the port — fisherman, then factory worker. He wasn't a man of many words. But what he said was precise. There was no room for misunderstanding in his house.

When I work at the salt flats, and write my quality reports — numbers, measurements, data — I think it's the same character. Reality is what it is. Numbers don't lie. The salt is good or it isn't. No grey areas in my work. And in my city.

My mother was different — more patient, more inclined to explain things at length, to give people the benefit of the doubt. I'm somewhere between the two. At work I'm my father: numbers, measurements, facts. At home I try to be a bit more like my mother. I'm not always successful.

The city has changed in the time I've been alive. There used to be more industry — the petrochemical plant on the outskirts employed a lot of people, then it was decommissioned and the employment went with it. Some young people left. Some stayed and found other work. The ones who stayed tend to have the characteristic directness of the place — they made a clear decision and they stand by it. The ones who left tend to come back for summer and say they miss it. I believe them. I also think they made the right choice for themselves. There's no contradiction there. That's the kind of thing you can say here without people taking offence. We're not sentimental about it. You go where your life takes you.

But for me, the six o'clock shift is still the best. The salt is still here. The flamingos are still here. The gulf is still here. What else would I need.

CAP. 20 — FINALE: ANTONELLO SAYS GOODBYE
voice: valerio

At half past seven I finish my first round of checks. I stop at the edge of the crystallisation basin — the furthest one, where the salt has already formed and is waiting to be harvested.

The sun is up now. The flamingos are still in the central basins, several hundred of them. The water reflects the Gargano. Behind me, a few kilometres away, Siponto lies under the sand and Manfredonia is waking up.

Every morning the same and every morning different. That's the secret of salt: it's always the same, but every crystal is unique.

I write the last number in my notebook. I close it. I'll type up the report at the desk inside, where it's cooler. But first I stand here for another minute. I do this every morning — just stand and look.

The flamingos are moving slowly across the basin surface, their long necks down, the pink of their plumage deepening as the sun gets higher. The Gargano to the north is catching the morning light on its cliffs — white limestone flashing. The salt that formed overnight on the edges of the crystallisation basin is catching the light too, small crystals glinting. Two different kinds of white in the same field of view.

Thirty years of this and I still can't explain exactly why I find it beautiful. I have the technical vocabulary for the chemistry, the biology, the geology. I don't have the vocabulary for what it is to stand at the edge of a salt basin at half past seven in the morning and feel that the world is in the right order. But that's what I feel.

This gulf has other stories.

The villages perched inland, where a park ranger knows every path and every fire, the forgotten coast between Rodi and Peschici where the road climbs past unnamed panoramas, the monks of Siponto who prayed where pagans had prayed before them, the islands where a boatman used to take Lucio Dalla into the violet caves. Each one has someone who tells it. Someone who never left.

The Gargano doesn't end here.
