## AUDIO SCRIPT — LOCOROTONDO GUIDE (EN)
*Voice: Francesco, 50 years old, winemaker of the round village*
*Format: mixed car + walking · 25 minutes*

---

### CH. 1 — INTRO: THE ITRIA VALLEY FROM THE WINDOW
*(in the car, approaching Locorotondo)*

You're climbing.

You've just left the plain and you're heading up toward Locorotondo. Four hundred and ten metres above sea level — not a mountain, but up here on this hill the wind always blows, and in summer while the rest of Puglia is baking, this place stays cool.

Look down into the valley as you climb. Trulli scattered between the olive trees, vineyards, stone walls dividing the fields into irregular patches. This is the Itria Valley — a natural basin inside the Murge plateau where for centuries people have farmed, built, lived.

My name is Francesco. I'm fifty years old, I was born here, and I run my farm here — wine, olive oil, and something else I'll tell you about later. I know every piece of this land. And now I'm going to tell you about it.

Locorotondo is not Alberobello. You won't find the iconic trulli, the crowds of tourists with selfie sticks, souvenir shops open until eleven at night. What you'll find here is a village that lived for a thousand years without needing to prove anything to anyone. And it shows.

---

### CH. 2 — LOCUS ROTUNDUS: THE VILLAGE THAT GOES ROUND
*(on foot, entering the historic centre)*

The name says it all: Locorotondo. From the Latin *locus rotundus* — round place.

This isn't a metaphor. The historic centre is literally round. The houses are arranged in concentric rings around the central church, like ripples when you drop a stone in water. This layout has been documented since the twelfth century — it's not an accident, not an urban coincidence. It was a deliberate choice by the people who built this place.

The origins go back to around the year 1000. First a fief of the Benedictine monastery of Monopoli, then of the Knights of St John of Jerusalem, then of the Aragonese. In the 1400s it was part of the Principality of Taranto. The final name "Locorotondo" was only adopted in 1834 — before that it was called Casale San Giorgio, then Casale Rotondo, then Luogo Rotondo. A thousand years of history, four different names, the same round shape.

Walk without a plan for ten minutes. Follow the curved streets. You'll notice you're going in circles — literally. You don't get lost in Locorotondo, because sooner or later you come back to where you started.

---

### CH. 3 — THE CUMMERSE: NOT TRULLI
*(on foot, in the historic centre)*

Look at the rooftops.

You won't find the cones of trulli here. You'll find double-pitched roofs, sloping, covered with thin limestone slabs called *chiancarelle*. These houses are called *cummerse*. The word probably comes from the Latin *cum vertice* — with a peak. And they predate the trulli in an urban context — the earliest ones date to the fourteenth century.

Why do trulli have cone roofs and cummerse have pitched roofs? Because it rains more here.

The Murge of the Trulli is more exposed to wind and rainfall than the plain below. A cone roof doesn't drain well — it works fine where there's little rain. A double-pitched roof channels water down the sides, straight into the cisterns. Practical, efficient, designed for this exact climate.

Cummerse are two-storey houses with very steep internal stairs. Thick walls, small windows. Built to stay cool in summer and shelter from the wind in winter. Every construction detail answers a real problem.

When I see a tourist photographing the rooftops of Locorotondo and thinking they're shooting something decorative, I want to tell them: you're photographing centuries of practical engineering. Every single limestone slab is there for a reason.

---

### CH. 4 — THE WHITE AND THE LIME
*(on foot, among the whitewashed houses)*

Now you understand why they call it the white village.

Lime everywhere. Walls, steps, door frames. A white so uniform that in full sun it's almost blinding. And it's not just about looks.

Lime is antibacterial. Before disinfectants existed, whitewashing walls with lime was how you kept things clean, kept disease away. All across southern Italy people did this — but in Locorotondo this tradition stayed more alive than elsewhere, and it became part of the identity of the place.

Every year, before the patron saint's festival, families would whitewash the facades. Not for tourists. For the village, for the community, for the sense of order and respect you owe the place where you live.

People still do it, at least in part. And the result is this white that never gets tired — because it's not artificial, not paint, not uniform like an industrial colour. It's lime on limestone. Every wall absorbs light in a slightly different way.

---

### CH. 5 — THE VINEYARD: VERDECA AND BIANCO D'ALESSANO
*(on foot or heading back toward the car, with a view over the countryside)*

Now let me tell you about what I do.

I prefer not to name my farm, because this isn't advertising: we're here to understand the land, not to sell bottles. I make wine, olive oil, and something else — but let's start with the wine.

The Locorotondo DOC has existed since June 10th, 1969. It's one of the oldest recognised white wines in Puglia. Two grape varieties: Verdeca and Bianco d'Alessano.

These are native varieties — they've been growing here for centuries, selected over generations by farmers who understood which grapes adapt best to this land. Verdeca likes deep, cool soils — the valley floor. Bianco d'Alessano is tougher, it grows well on the poor, sun-drenched hillsides where you wouldn't plant anything else.

One gives the wine its fragrance and flavour. The other gives it structure and body. Together they make a wine that's pale straw yellow — sometimes with a greenish tinge — delicate on the nose, dry on the palate, with a slightly bitter finish.

It's not a wine that shouts. It's a wine that speaks quietly. But once you understand what it's saying, you don't want to stop listening.

The secret isn't in a modern winery, or selected yeasts, or technology. The secret is in the vineyard. A Verdeca vine that's put down twenty years of roots into this limestone hillside knows things no winemaking textbook has ever written down.

---

### CH. 6 — THE OLIVE OIL AND THE LAVENDER
*(on foot or driving through the countryside)*

Two things I produce that I want to tell you about, because most people have no idea how they're actually made.

The olive oil. The Itria Valley has been olive country for thousands of years. The olive trees here don't get planted — they're already there, some of them hundreds of years old. The milling of the olives — extracting the oil — has to happen fast, within a few hours of picking, before the olives oxidize and the oil loses quality. Every hour between harvest and press is one more hour of acidity in the oil. That's why good olive oil costs what it costs.

The lavender. This is the one that surprises people most.

Lavender loves exactly the kind of soil we have here: limestone, dry, well-drained, sunny. It's not an exotic plant for Puglia — it grows beautifully on this hillside.

The essential oil is extracted by steam distillation — the flower heads go into the still, steam passes through them and carries the aromatic compounds with it, then condenses and separates from the water. Temperature controlled below ninety-five degrees Celsius, otherwise the most delicate fragrances break down.

The yields are tiny. For one litre of essential oil from Lavandula angustifolia — the finest variety, with the most refined scent — you need roughly two thousand kilograms of flowers. That's not a typo. It's why a genuine essential oil, made this way, cannot cost three euros.

When you smell lavender and think "beautiful" — now you know how much work went into that smell.

---

### CH. 7 — THE VIEW: BALCONY OF THE VALLEY
*(at the belvedere or panoramic point)*

Stop here for a moment.

Look down.

The whole Itria Valley is below you. The trulli of Alberobello to one side, Martina Franca to the other, farmhouses scattered between the fields, rows of vines, olive groves that go on forever.

This is why Locorotondo was built on this hill. Not for the view — though it's beautiful. For control. From here you can see everything moving in the valley. You can see who's coming, where it's raining, where the fields are burning. For centuries, this view was information. It was power.

Now it's a viewing terrace where tourists take photos.

But try to see it the way someone saw it a thousand years ago — no camera, no phone. With the responsibility of protecting the people who lived below. Knowing that this limestone under your feet was everything they had.

Changes how you see it, doesn't it?

---

### CH. 8 — CLOSING: GIACOMO SAYS GOODBYE
*(on foot or getting back in the car)*

So — Locorotondo.

A village that's been going in circles for a thousand years, whitewashed houses with pitched roofs built for the rain, a white wine that tastes of limestone and wind, an olive oil made from ancient trees, and a lavender that smells of patience.

It's not a place that overwhelms you. It's a place that gets inside you slowly, if you give it the time.

If you want to take something concrete home: stop at one of the wineries in the village centre, pick up a bottle of Locorotondo DOC, and drink it that same evening — cold, with something simple. Bread, cheese, olives. Everything becomes clear.

And if you want to keep moving, Martina Franca is ten minutes away. A completely different city — baroque, musical, with a history you won't expect.

See you there.
