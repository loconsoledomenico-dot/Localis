GARGANO — UMBRA FOREST, MONTE SANT'ANGELO, SAN GIOVANNI ROTONDO
Voice: Fra' Salvatore, 73 years old, friar of the Sanctuary of Monte Sant'Angelo, born in San Giovanni Rotondo

=====
CAP. 1 — INTRO: THREE PLACES, ONE PATH
voice: valerio

These aren't three destinations.

They're a path.

The forest, the grotto, the monastery. Nature, the archangel, the saint. Three things that appear separate on a map but on this promontory form something unlike anywhere else in the world.

My name is Fra' Salvatore. Seventy-three years old. I was born in San Giovanni Rotondo in 1952 — four years before Padre Pio died. My parents would bring me to his mass every Sunday. I saw him as a child. I don't remember — but my parents used to say he was a small man with gloves on his hands and a presence you felt before you saw him. The air would change in the church when he walked in. That's what my mother said. She wasn't a woman who exaggerated.

For thirty years I have served the Sanctuary of Monte Sant'Angelo. I walk the Umbra Forest every week. I know this land the way you know a book you've read hundreds of times — and you still find new things in it, each time, if you read slowly enough.

I'll tell you what I see. Not the version written for tourist guides. The real one. The one that lives in the rock and the trees, in the cold air of the grotto and the silence before dawn.

=====
CAP. 2 — THE UMBRA FOREST: THE PREPARATION
voice: valerio

Before going to the Archangel's grotto, go into the forest.

It wasn't my idea. It's what medieval pilgrims did. They arrived on foot from the Apennines, crossed the forest, descended to Monte Sant'Angelo. The forest was the preparation. The passage between the ordinary world and the sacred place.

The Umbra Forest is eleven thousand hectares of primeval woodland. Beeches, oaks, yews, hollies — trees no one has ever systematically cut down. Centuries of free growth. Trunks two metres across. Branches so high that sunlight never reaches the ground. In the middle of summer, in the deepest part of the forest, you walk in green shadow. The light filters down through layers of leaves and arrives at your feet as something dim and cool, like light through cathedral glass.

When I enter the forest, I always stop at the same spot — where the trees close over the path and everything suddenly turns to silence and shadow. It's a threshold. I feel it physically. As if you were stepping into a different space. The sounds of the road, the cars, the voices — they disappear. What remains is the creak of the branches, the low rushing of the wind through the canopy, sometimes the far call of a bird.

Medieval pilgrims had a name for it. They called it the sacred wood. Because they understood — certain forests are not just trees. They are places with their own logic, their own time, their own silence. You don't impose yourself on a forest like this. You enter it on its terms.

I've seen people come in talking loudly and fall quiet after ten minutes. Not because anyone asked them. Because the place itself asked them. That is what I mean when I say preparation.

=====
CAP. 3 — THE ANIMALS OF THE FOREST
voice: valerio

The forest is full of life.

Red deer, fallow deer, roe deer, wild boar, foxes, badgers. Dozens of bird species — the black woodpecker, the peregrine falcon, the hen harrier. And the otter, which has returned to the streams of the Gargano after decades of absence.

I always walk in the early morning. Half past five, six o'clock. When the animals are still moving and the hikers haven't arrived yet. At that hour the forest smells different. The dew is still on the leaves. The ground is soft. Every step leaves a print. You read the trails of the animals in the earth — the slots of the deer, the rootings of the boar, the small precise prints of the fox.

I've seen the stag cross the path in front of me three times in thirty years. Each time it stopped, looked at me, then disappeared into the trees. A long look, unhurried. No fear in it — more like recognition. I don't know if it was the same animal each time. It doesn't matter.

The forest is a place of contemplation. Not of sport. Not of fitness. Those who run through it have no idea what they've just passed through. Those who walk it slowly, in silence, come out different from how they went in. I believe this. I've seen it happen too many times to doubt it.

The sounds you hear in the forest, if you stop walking and just stand still — that is the real lesson. The forest speaks constantly. Most people never hear it.

=====
CAP. 4 — MONTE SANT'ANGELO: THE GROTTO
voice: valerio

Monte Sant'Angelo sits at six hundred and ninety-six metres above sea level.

You get there from the forest or from the sea — by car, today. Once, people came on foot. Pilgrims from Ireland, from Germany, from Spain. They walked for weeks. Months. They carried illness, grief, impossible requests. They came with the hope that the Archangel would hear what no human could answer.

The Sanctuary of San Michele Arcangelo is the second oldest Christian pilgrimage site in the world. The first is Jerusalem. The second is here. This is not civic pride — it is history, documented and recognised.

Tradition holds that the Archangel Michael appeared in this grotto in 490 AD. Three apparitions in three different years. During the third, on September 29th, 493, he left the mark of his cloak on the rock. The grotto became a sanctuary. No human hands built it or consecrated it — and that is the point. That is what drew pilgrims across entire continents. A place not made by men.

When you descend the eighty-six steps and enter the cavern, the first thing you feel is the cold. Twelve degrees all year round, summer and winter alike. The cold is immediate, physical, clean. After the heat of the Gargano summer outside, it enters the lungs like water. Then your eyes adjust to the darkness and you see the statue of the Archangel on the altar — gilded bronze, wings spread wide, sword raised. The gilding catches the candlelight and gives the whole grotto a soft amber glow, like something seen at the edge of a dream.

Fifteen hundred years of prayer in this rock. You feel it. It isn't suggestion. It's something physical. Something in the stone has accumulated over centuries — the weight of all those who came before you, with their grief and their faith, and knelt in exactly the same place.

=====
CAP. 5 — THE PILGRIMS: WHO CAME AND WHY
voice: valerio

Charlemagne stopped here in the year 787.

Not in passing — he came deliberately. He walked the route from the plain up to the grotto on foot. An emperor descending on foot, like any ordinary pilgrim. I think about that sometimes. The most powerful man in the Western world, removing his boots, placing his feet on the same stone as the poorest farmer from the valley. The grotto levelled them. It still does.

Francis of Assisi came in 1216. But he stopped outside. He wouldn't enter — he considered himself too much of a sinner to enter a place consecrated by the Archangel himself. He remained kneeling on the threshold. As a penance, he carved a cross into the rock. That cross is still visible today. A man who built churches, who spoke to birds, who stripped himself of everything — and still felt unworthy of this place. I find that moving. Not because it proves anything. Because it shows the quality of the man.

The Normans, when they conquered southern Italy in the eleventh century, venerated Michael the Archangel as the protector of their enterprise. They built the outer bell tower that you can still see today. The stone tower visible from the entire valley below. They were warriors building a church. That combination — the sword and the altar — is very much the spirit of this place.

The Lombards before them considered Michael the protector of battle. Every Lombard warrior leaving for war passed through this grotto first. As people still do today — in different forms, but they still do. They come with their own battles. Not armies and kingdoms. But illness, loss, fear. The battles change. The need to ask for protection does not.

CAP. 6 — SAN GIOVANNI ROTONDO: PADRE PIO
voice: valerio

San Giovanni Rotondo is thirty-five kilometres from Monte Sant'Angelo.

It is the sanctuary of Padre Pio. Seven million pilgrims every year. The second most visited Catholic pilgrimage site in the world, after Lourdes.

I was born in this town. I've lived here my whole life. And I must be honest with you — what you see today bears little resemblance to the place where Padre Pio lived.

Padre Pio arrived in San Giovanni Rotondo in 1916. The town was poor, isolated, hard to reach. There wasn't even a paved road. Pilgrims arrived on mule-back. The convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie stood on the hill with its few cells, its chapel, its garden. A small enclosed world. When I imagine him there — the cold of the Gargano winter, the silence of the convent before dawn, the long hours of prayer — I understand something about him that the new buildings can't tell you.

Today there's the new Sanctuary — designed by Renzo Piano, inaugurated in 2004, capable of holding thirty thousand people. It's large, it's modern, it's necessary given the numbers. The architecture is serious work. Piano understood that a sacred building must have humility as well as grandeur, and he found a way to give the structure that double quality.

But the place worth seeing is the old church — the small one, where Padre Pio celebrated mass in the morning. Where people waited for hours to confess to him. That church still has that quality — small, intimate, human. You can feel in the proportions of the room why it mattered. You understand in your body what the new sanctuary, for all its beauty, cannot quite replicate.

He received the stigmata in 1918. He bore them for fifty years. He died in 1968 — and that morning, witnesses say, the stigmata had vanished. The skin was intact. I don't know what to make of that. I only know it was reported by people present at his death, people who had seen those wounds for decades.

CAP. 7 — WHO PADRE PIO WAS: BEYOND RELIGIOUS TOURISM
voice: valerio

Let me tell you what my parents used to say about him.

My mother went to his five o'clock mass. Summer and winter alike. In any weather. She would queue outside the church from midnight. She brought a blanket in winter. She brought bread and ate standing up, waiting. She did this for years.

She said it wasn't the mass itself. It was him. The way he celebrated — slowly, as if every word had weight. He would pause between the words of the liturgy and the pause itself carried something. Sometimes he would stop and weep at the altar. Not theatrically — quietly, like a man remembering something painful. Sometimes the mass lasted three hours. No one left.

People brought him letters with their petitions. Healings, protection, graces. He read them, replied, prayed. He received hundreds of letters a day. My mother sent him two, in her life. He replied to both. Brief replies, a few lines each. She kept them until she died. I have them now, in a drawer.

He was also severe. In confession he turned away people who weren't ready. He had no patience for those who came out of curiosity. He could tell the difference — or so the people who were there believed, and I have no reason to doubt them. Curiosity and need look different, when you've spent decades looking at people's faces.

My father went once, to confess to him. He never told me what Padre Pio said to him. But he came back different. I could see it in his face when he walked through the door. Something had been set down. Something heavy, that he had been carrying a long time. He was another man. He lived twenty more years after that, and he was easier in himself, calmer. Whatever happened in that confessional, it worked.

=====
CAP. 8 — THE CONNECTION BETWEEN THE THREE PLACES
voice: valerio

There is something few people notice.

The Umbra Forest, Monte Sant'Angelo, San Giovanni Rotondo — all three sit on the same promontory. Less than forty kilometres apart.

The forest runs through the middle, like a green spine. Monte Sant'Angelo stands on the heights to the east, facing the Adriatic. San Giovanni Rotondo lies on the western side, facing the Tavoliere plain — the flat agricultural land of inland Puglia stretching toward the horizon. Two sanctuaries on opposite sides of the same ridge, with the ancient forest between them. As if the geography itself were an argument.

Medieval pilgrims visited all three. It wasn't religious tourism — it was a route. A spiritual itinerary with a logic. They began at Monte Sant'Angelo because the Archangel was older. They crossed the forest. They descended toward the plain. The landscape itself told them where to go.

Today you can do it by car in a single day. But the meaning is the same — these are not three separate things. They are one single experience. Nature, ancient sacred, recent sacred. The Gargano gives you all three in the same day. Three layers of time. The geological time of the forest, which measures itself in centuries of tree growth. The historical time of the Archangel's sanctuary, which measures itself in fifteen hundred years of pilgrimage. And the living memory of Padre Pio, which is still within the reach of people alive today.

My mother touched all three. In the same week, more than once. She understood this promontory as a unity. I learned it from her.

CAP. 9 — PRACTICAL ADVICE
voice: valerio

For the Umbra Forest: go in the early morning. Before nine. The car parks fill up fast in summer. The best trails are the long ones — at least two hours of walking. Not the quick loop from the car park. That isn't the forest. That's the edge of the forest. The forest begins twenty minutes in, when the last sound of the road disappears.

Wear shoes you don't mind getting dirty. After rain the trails are soft and the roots are slippery. In summer the forest is cooler than the coast — ten degrees cooler, sometimes more. You don't need a jacket in July, but in October bring one.

For Monte Sant'Angelo: avoid the midday hours in summer. The Archangel's Grotto is open all day, but the heat outside is unbearable from eleven until four. Early morning or late afternoon — the light is different, the crowds are thinner. The village itself is worth time. Walk the streets of the historic centre. The stone buildings, the narrow passages, the views over the gulf from the belvedere. Do not only visit the grotto and leave.

Cover your shoulders and knees to enter the sanctuary. This is still an active place of worship. The dress code is not bureaucracy — it is respect. Most people understand this when they step inside and see others praying.

For San Giovanni Rotondo: visit the old church before the new sanctuary. Feel the difference in scale. Understand where everything began. Sit for a few minutes in the small nave. Then see the new one — Renzo Piano's architecture deserves a moment of genuine attention, not a glance from the car park. But the old one first. Always the old one first.

CAP. 10 — THE UMBRA FOREST: THE ANIMALS YOU DON'T SEE
voice: valerio

The Umbra Forest is full of animals.

Almost no one sees them.

Not because they aren't there — they are, and in significant numbers. But the forest is dense, silent, and animals know when humans are nearby. They hear your footsteps before you're aware of them. They smell you before they hear you. By the time you arrive at the place where they were, they're already somewhere else.

The fallow deer of the Gargano are an indigenous subspecies — the Gargano fallow deer, smaller and darker than those on the mainland. There are also wild boar — many of them, and not always shy. Encountering them on a trail is not uncommon. They usually bolt. Usually. A mother with young is a different matter. You give her space and she gives you the same.

The beeches of the Umbra Forest are centuries old. Some are over five hundred years old. The yews — the trees, not the animals — are even older. A yew can live a thousand years. Walking alongside one is an act of humility. You are passing beside something that was already old when your entire family line began. That changes the scale of things. It shrinks you in a way that is not unpleasant. You feel small, and you feel correctly sized.

At the right season — late autumn, early spring — you might hear the bark of the deer at dawn. A sharp, rough sound, nothing like what you'd expect from an elegant animal. It carries across the whole forest. I've heard it hundreds of times and it still makes me stop and listen.

=====
CAP. 11 — MONTE SANT'ANGELO AND THE LOMBARDS
voice: valerio

The sanctuary of Monte Sant'Angelo has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2011.

Not on its own — together with other Lombard sites across Italy. The recognition is for the "Longobards in Italy: Places of Power." Monte Sant'Angelo is among them because something important began here.

The Lombards arrived in Italy in 568. The Duke of Benevento, in the seventh century, spread the cult of Saint Michael throughout Lombard Europe. From this grotto grew a network of sanctuaries dedicated to the archangel — Mont Saint-Michel in Normandy, the Sacra di San Michele in Piedmont. All connected to this rock. The cult radiated outward from here across the continent, following the paths of traders, soldiers, monks, pilgrims.

What I find remarkable is the continuity. The same place, the same grotto, the same stone — venerated without interruption for fifteen hundred years. No wars closed it permanently. No conquests ended the pilgrimage. The Byzantines held this land, then the Lombards, then the Normans, then the Swabians. Power changed hands many times. The grotto stayed open. That is not nothing. That is extraordinary.

When I stand inside the cavern and look at the altar, I sometimes try to count backward. Not the years — that's just arithmetic. But the people. The mothers who brought sick children. The soldiers who prayed before battle. The penitents, the desperate, the curious, the devout. Fifteen centuries of them. There is no number large enough to hold that.

=====
CAP. 12 — PADRE PIO: THE INVESTIGATIONS AND THE CANONIZATION
voice: valerio

I don't often say these things.

But the story of Padre Pio is not only devotion. It is also a story of investigations, of doubts, of a man who was suspected and scrutinized by the very Church that would later canonize him.

The stigmata appeared in 1918. The Vatican sent doctors and commissions. For years Padre Pio was forbidden from celebrating mass in public. Church authorities were divided — some believed, some did not. He spent years restricted, observed, questioned. A man of prayer living under suspicion. I think about what that would feel like — to believe entirely in what you are, and to be doubted by your own institution. He endured it without public bitterness. That, too, tells you something about him.

John Paul II beatified him in 1999. He canonized him in 2002. Today he is one of the most venerated saints in the world. Over seven million pilgrims a year to San Giovanni Rotondo.

I have my own faith. But I think every pilgrim must bring their own, not mine. What I can tell you is what I observed in the people around me who lived alongside Padre Pio. They were not credulous people. My parents were farm people. Practical. Not given to fantasy. And they believed in him completely — not because they were told to, but because they saw something with their own eyes that they could not otherwise explain. That testimony means something to me, even if it proves nothing to anyone else.

=====
CAP. 13 — THE PILGRIMS' RITUAL
voice: valerio

Every day, dozens of coaches arrive at San Giovanni Rotondo.

The pilgrims step off carrying empty bottles. They go to the blessed-water fountains. They fill them. They enter the church. They pray before the body of the saint. They buy the souvenirs. They get back on the coach.

It is a ritual that has repeated identically for decades. No different from the medieval pilgrims who came on foot from half of Europe — the transport has changed, the impulse has not. The empty bottle brought to be filled. The journey made not for leisure but for need. The return home carrying something that was not there before. These forms are ancient.

Simple people come, educated people, desperate people, curious people. Everyone brings something. Everyone seeks something. On this promontory, something draws faith like a magnet.

I have seen people arrive skeptical and leave silent. Not converted — I don't mean that. Just quieter. As if something they were carrying had become lighter without them deciding to put it down. I don't have an explanation for that. I only know that I've seen it happen repeatedly, and that it happens more here than in most places I've been. Perhaps it's the accumulated faith of generations, sitting in the stones. Perhaps it's something else entirely. I stopped needing to explain it a long time ago.

=====
CAP. 14 — HOW TO PLAN THE ROUTE
voice: valerio

To visit all three places in one day, you need a car.

By car the logical route is: morning in the Umbra Forest, midday at Monte Sant'Angelo, afternoon in San Giovanni Rotondo. About three and a half hours of driving in total, spread across the whole day.

Leave early. The forest in the morning, before the heat builds, is a different place from the forest at noon. Arrive at Monte Sant'Angelo before midday — the grotto will be busy but manageable. The old village around the sanctuary deserves an hour of walking. Eat lunch there, in one of the restaurants near the sanctuary. The lamb is good. So is the tarallo dolce, the sweet glazed ring — you'll find it in every bakery on the main street.

Then drive west to San Giovanni Rotondo in the afternoon. The drive itself crosses the spine of the Gargano — you pass through the forest again, from the eastern side to the western. The light changes as you cross. The landscape opens from the closed green of the forest to the wider, flatter light of the Tavoliere side.

For the Umbra Forest: wear proper footwear. In summer the forest is cooler than the coast, but the humidity still finds you. For Monte Sant'Angelo: cover your shoulders and knees. For San Giovanni Rotondo: go on a weekday if you can. Weekends are overcrowded. The old church is more intimate than the new one. But Renzo Piano's architecture deserves a moment. The old one first.

CAP. 15 — THE GROTTO: HOW YOU DESCEND
voice: valerio

The sanctuary of San Michele is not at the top of the mountain.

It is inside the mountain.

You descend a staircase — eighty-six steps, cut into the rock, going down. The air cools with every step. Not gradually — in stages, as you pass through layers of the mountain. Five steps in you feel it on your arms. Twenty steps in and your shirt is no longer enough. By the time you reach the grotto, the temperature is around twelve degrees all year round.

The light fades with the depth. The sounds of the outside world — voices, traffic, the noise of the village market — cut off cleanly. What remains is your own breath, your footsteps on the stone, and the distant sound of prayer from below.

The grotto has been venerated since the time of the Lombards — perhaps before. On the wall to the left of the altar there is an impression in the rock. Tradition says it is the imprint of Saint Michael's cloak from the third apparition. In 490 AD. I will not tell you what to make of it. I only tell you it is there, and that for fifteen centuries people have come specifically to see it.

I've entered it hundreds of times. I never get used to the sudden cold, the way the silence changes, the smell of ancient stone. That smell — damp limestone, old candle wax, the mineral trace of water seeping through the rock — is not unpleasant. It is the smell of depth and time. Even those who don't believe feel something, in that place. I don't know what to call it — perhaps it's just the rock and the darkness and the accumulated quiet of fifteen centuries. But it's something. You come out of the grotto and the ordinary daylight seems louder than it did before.

=====
CAP. 16 — THE FOOD OF THE SACRED GARGANO
voice: valerio

After the grotto, after the pilgrims, after the prayers — you eat.

Monte Sant'Angelo has its own dishes. The sugar-glazed tarallo — sweet, crunchy, eaten for centuries as a votive offering and as a snack. A small thing, simple, but made correctly it has a quality that stays with you. The focaccia with tomato — the real kind, with raw tomato on top, oil poured generously, sometimes a pinch of oregano. Not the oil-soaked thick focaccia of Bari — lighter, with a crispness underneath.

In the restaurants around the sanctuary you'll find lamb — the Gargano has always been a land of shepherds and transhumance. Flocks crossed this promontory for centuries, following the paths between the mountain and the plain. The meat carries that history. Grilled lamb, or braised with olives, is what has always been eaten here.

The Gargano also produces good olive oil — intensely flavored, with a slight bitterness that is not a defect but a character. And the bread. The bread of the Gargano interior is made with durum wheat, baked in wood-fired ovens, with a crust that holds. You break it rather than cut it.

I eat little — monastic life doesn't accustom you to abundance. But when I can, I stop at the village market. The cheeses of the Gargano — cacioricotta, smoked scamorza — are simple things made well. You taste the grass in them, the particular flavour of these hills. Simplicity is a form of respect, in the kitchen as in prayer. The best food here is not elaborate. It is precise.

CAP. 17 — THE UMBRA FOREST: TRAILS AND SEASONS
voice: valerio

The Umbra Forest changes with the seasons — and each season is radically different from the last.

In spring the undergrowth bursts open. Flowers, fresh green, birds singing from dawn until dusk. The streams run full. The soil is dark and soft. New ferns uncurl along the trail edges. It's the best time to walk — cool but not cold, bright but not blinding. The forest is in full voice. Every layer of it is alive simultaneously.

In summer the forest means shade and coolness — a refuge when the coast is at thirty-eight degrees. Many people come for exactly that. But bring water — the trails have no fountains. And don't underestimate the distances. What looks short on the map feels long on foot, on trails that rise and fall with the terrain.

In autumn the beeches turn red and orange. If you come in October, the woodland looks painted. That's not rhetoric. It really is. The light through the autumn leaves has a particular quality — warm, amber, almost liquid. The floor of the forest is deep in fallen leaves, and walking through them makes a sound that I find one of the most satisfying sounds in the world. Dry leaves underfoot, the smell of earth turning, the cold coming down.

In winter the forest is nearly deserted. Snow often falls on Monte Sant'Angelo — something the people on the coast find hard to believe. But the Gargano is high, and the cold comes. The bare beeches, snow between the branches, total silence — that is the forest I love most. The structure of the trees is visible then. The bones of the forest. You see what the leaves, in other seasons, hide. The winter forest is the most honest one.

=====
CAP. 18 — SAN MARCO IN LAMIS AND THE SPIRITUAL GARGANO
voice: valerio

There is another place on this Gargano that deserves a mention.

San Marco in Lamis.

A town in the interior, less known than the others. But with a Franciscan monastery — the convent of Sant'Antonio di Padova — that was already a place of spiritual life before Padre Pio. And with an Easter procession, the Processione delle Fracchie, one of the oldest and most intense in all of Puglia.

The fracchie are tree trunks that are burned in procession on the night of Good Friday. Enormous torches — sometimes three metres tall. The town descends into the streets in silence, carrying these flames. The heat, the smoke, the darkness all around. The light from the burning wood throws long shadows. You feel in the middle of something very old — older than any single tradition, reaching back to the way fire has always meant mourning and vigil. It is something very ancient, and it has survived because it answers something real in the people who carry it.

The Gargano isn't only sea and beaches. It is also this — fire, silence, faith, night. The interior of this promontory has a character entirely different from its coast. The coast is loud and luminous. The interior is private. It holds its history quietly. I was born in the interior and I have lived my life in it. I prefer it to the coast. Not because the coast is not beautiful — it is, profoundly. But the interior is where the Gargano keeps its oldest self.

=====
CAP. 19 — THE GARGANO FROM ABOVE: THE VIEW FROM MONTE SANT'ANGELO
voice: valerio

From Monte Sant'Angelo you can see the entire gulf.

The town sits at eight hundred metres — high, above everything. From the belvedere you can see Manfredonia below, the gulf opening toward Bari, the salt flats of Margherita shining like a distant mirror. On clear days you can see the Tremiti Islands — green shapes on the horizon, barely distinct from the sea. The distance makes them look weightless, suspended. It is one of those views that makes you recalibrate your sense of scale.

It is one of the most beautiful views in Puglia. No photograph does it justice. You have to be there — feel the cold air even in summer, that wind that comes up from the gulf and finds you on the belvedere and makes you reach for something to hold. Listen to the silence above the wind. Look out as far as the eye can reach.

Medieval pilgrims climbed for days on foot to arrive here. They carried illness, grief, impossible requests. Then they saw this view. I understand why they felt they had arrived at God's doorstep. With that effort in their bodies, and that beauty before their eyes — it must have been an experience beyond description. The exhaustion and the view arriving simultaneously, each making the other more powerful.

I come to this belvedere alone, sometimes at dusk. The light changes very fast at that hour. The gulf turns from blue to silver to a dark grey-green. The lights of Manfredonia begin to appear, one by one, down on the plain. And the islands, if the air is clear, hold their colour longer than everything else. I stand there until I'm cold. Then I go back in.

CAP. 20 — SILENCE AS PRACTICE
voice: valerio

I live in a place where silence is a practice, not a coincidence.

Monastic life imposes hours of silence. Not silence because there's nothing to say — silence because silence has value in itself. Listening without producing noise. Being present without taking up space. It sounds simple and it is extraordinarily difficult. Most people haven't sat in silence for ten consecutive minutes in years. The modern world works very hard to prevent it.

When I accompany visitors to the grotto, I sometimes ask them to stay silent for five minutes. Not everyone manages. Someone looks at their phone. Someone starts talking after thirty seconds. But someone really stops. They stand in the cold and the dark and they stop doing anything. And when they come out, I can see it in their face — something has loosened. Some tightness, some held thing, has released slightly.

I don't know if it's the grotto. I don't know if it's the cold, the stone, the darkness. I know that in certain places silence feels different. Heavier. More inhabited. And this is one of those places. The silence in the grotto has texture. It is not the silence of an empty room. It is a silence that has been built up over centuries, layer by layer, the way sediment builds on the floor of a lake. You feel the weight of it. And instead of being oppressive, it is — for most people who let it happen — a relief. As if the silence were holding something they didn't have to hold anymore.

=====
CAP. 21 — CLOSING: FRA' SALVATORE SAYS GOODBYE
voice: valerio

Seventy-three years old. Born on this promontory. I've never really left it.

People ask me if I don't get tired of always being here. Of seeing the same places every day.

No.

Because every day these places are different. The forest changes with the seasons. The grotto changes with the light — in the morning the candles are freshly lit and the amber glow is different from the afternoon, when they've burned lower. The sanctuary changes with the people who enter it — every day someone arrives who has never been here, and I see on their face the first time. The surprise of the cold, the adjustment of the eyes to the darkness, the moment when they look up and see the Archangel on the altar. That expression. It never grows old.

I'll leave you with something my mother told me when I was young.

She said: the Lord made this place different from the others. I don't know why — perhaps because of the rock, perhaps because of the sea, perhaps for a reason we can't understand. But he made it different. And you can feel it. She said this simply, the way she said most things. Without drama. As a fact she had verified personally, over many years, and found consistent.

She was right. You can still feel it.

This promontory has other faces.

The green sea of Vieste, the trabucchi clinging to the rock between Peschici and the coast, the islands where monks still live, the inland towns that look down over the lake from above. Each has someone to tell its story. Someone who never left.

The Gargano doesn't end here.
