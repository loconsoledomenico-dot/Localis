## AUDIO SCRIPT — ALBEROBELLO GUIDE (EN)
*Voice: Michele, 60 years old, Murgia farmer*
*Format: mixed car + walking · 25–30 minutes*

---

### CH. 1 — INTRO: THE MURGIA FROM THE WINDOW
*(in the car, approaching Alberobello)*

Take a look around while you're still in the car.

What you see out the window — these open fields, these gnarled olive trees, these stone walls running off in every direction — isn't just landscape. It's where the trulli come from. The same stone, the same technique, the same way of thinking.

We're on the Murge plateau. Limestone everywhere. Under your feet, above your feet, all around. This stone formed a hundred and thirty million years ago, when there was a sea here. The sea disappeared. The stone stayed. And people built on top of it with whatever they had.

See those dry stone walls along the fields? No cement, no mortar. Stone on stone, held together by balance alone. UNESCO — yes, that UNESCO in Paris — added them to the Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2018. Along with seven other European countries: Croatia, France, Greece, Spain and others. Because this technique was invented by every Mediterranean people, and passed down from father to son for thousands of years.

My name is Michele. I'm sixty years old, born here, spent my life working this land. And now I'm going to tell you about Alberobello. The real one — not the one on the postcards.

Park the car when you get there. And walk slowly.

---

### CH. 2 — WHAT IS A TRULLO, REALLY
*(in the car or on arrival, before entering the district)*

Before we go in, I want you to know something.

Everyone who comes here thinks they know what a trullo is. They've seen it in photos, on calendars, on someone's Instagram. But most people have no idea how it's actually built, or why.

A trullo is a dry stone structure made from local limestone. The stones are stacked using a technique called corbelling — each ring of stone overhangs the one below by a little, until both sides meet at the top and the circle closes. No glue. No cement. Just stone and geometry.

We locals called them *casedde*. Not "trulli." The word trullo probably comes from Greek, from a word meaning dome. But to my grandfather, they were the *casedde*, and that was that.

The stone came from here. When you dug a cistern to collect rainwater — and water here was gold — you pulled out limestone blocks. You didn't throw them away. You used them to build walls.

Now let me tell you why they built without mortar. This is where the story gets interesting.

We're in the 1500s. This land belongs to the Counts Acquaviva d'Aragona, lords of Conversano. The Count brings forty farming families from the town of Noci, gives them land to work, and takes a tenth of the harvest in return. Normal enough.

The problem is the King of Naples. There was a law — the Pragmatica De Baronibus — that said: if a baron founds a new settlement, he must ask the King's permission and pay tributes to the Crown. The Count had no interest in doing either.

Solution: don't build anything "permanent." If there's no mortar, technically the houses can be dismantled. If they can be dismantled, it's not a stable settlement. Not a village. And if it's not a village, the King doesn't need to know.

When the royal inspector was about to arrive, all you had to do was pull out the pinnacle at the top of the roof — that stone in the shape of a sphere or a cross that you see above every trullo — and the roof would collapse on its own into a pile of stones. No house, no village, no tributes.

That's the story they tell. It's plausible, consistent with the records of the time. There's no direct written proof, but the logic holds.

What is certain is that the Acquaviva family ruled Alberobello until May 27, 1797 — two hundred years of trulli without mortar — when seven representatives of the townspeople appeared before King Ferdinand IV of Bourbon in Taranto and won their freedom. From that day on, Alberobello became a royal city.

And from that day on, they could finally build with mortar.

---

### CH. 3 — RIONE MONTI: THE SHOWCASE
*(on foot, entering Rione Monti)*

You're inside now.

What you see around you is Rione Monti. Over a thousand trulli. The largest concentration of this type of architecture in the world. It's been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1996.

But before UNESCO — back in 1910 — Rione Monti was declared a National Monument. Eighty-six years before the international recognition, someone had already understood that this place was out of the ordinary.

Look carefully as you walk. Notice the difference between the trulli. Some have windows full of souvenirs, doors open onto shops, signs with prices in three languages. These are the ones the owners sold or rented out — often families who stopped living here decades ago.

Then there are the ones with shuttered windows, slightly darker stone, a pot of geraniums outside. These are the ones still lived in. They're not a tourist attraction. Someone's home.

Alberobello became a UNESCO site in 1996. Since then, property prices have risen, many residents have sold, and the historic center has gradually become what you see today. This isn't a criticism — it's what happens in every UNESCO site in the world. Venice, Dubrovnik, Toledo. Tourism saves the monuments and changes the lives of the people who live there.

You're not here to judge. You're here to see.

---

### CH. 4 — THE SYMBOLS ON THE PINNACLES
*(on foot, stopping to look at the roofs)*

Look up.

See those symbols painted in white lime on the rooftops? Crosses, hearts, stars, shapes you don't immediately recognize. Over two hundred different types have been catalogued. Some look Christian, some look like magic symbols, some are simply the owner's initials or a picture of what he grew — almonds, grapes, wheat.

The pinnacles — those stone shapes at the tip of each cone, sphere or cross or star — are different from trullo to trullo. Nobody knows for certain whether they were the master builder's signature, a family identification mark, or something symbolic.

Here's the thing: nobody really knows.

There are many interpretations. The Christian one: the sun represents Christ, the moon represents humanity. The magical one: zodiac symbols protect the house — Taurus brings abundance, Pisces protects against lightning. The practical one: they're just personal decorations, like a house number from another era.

Tourists want a precise answer. "What does that symbol mean?" And guides sometimes give them one. But the truth is that the historical documentation is incomplete. These symbols were painted by farmers, not notaries. They didn't leave written records.

The mystery is part of the trullo. Don't try to solve it — just look at it.

---

### CH. 5 — AIA PICCOLA: THE REAL ONE
*(on foot, crossing toward Rione Aia Piccola)*

Now I'm taking you somewhere almost nobody goes.

Cross the main road and head toward Rione Aia Piccola. About four hundred trulli, fewer shops, fewer tourists, more silence.

This is the district that still looks like what Alberobello was before UNESCO. Real families still live here. Children play outside, grandmothers sit on chairs in front of the door, the cat sleeps on the stone wall.

Life in a traditional trullo isn't what the brochure says. In summer they're cool inside — thick stone keeps the heat out. In winter, damp is a real problem, because stone without modern insulation absorbs moisture from the air. And without major structural work, you can't fit a proper plumbing system — the walls weren't built for it.

The people who still live here do so by choice, or out of necessity, or because that's their grandparents' house and they won't sell it at any price. And they have my full respect.

---

### CH. 6 — THE TRULLO SOVRANO
*(on foot, in front of the Trullo Sovrano)*

In front of you is the Trullo Sovrano. The only two-storey trullo in the world.

It's not the biggest, not the oldest, but it's the most extraordinary from a construction standpoint. The original core dates to the early 1600s. The main section was built in the first half of the 1700s for the Perta family — specifically for the priest Cataldo Perta, born 1744, died 1809, a man of the Church wealthy enough to have something no one else had.

The central dome is fourteen metres high. The complex has twelve cones in total. And inside, there's a stone staircase — completely non-standard in trulli — leading to the upper floor.

Another fundamental difference: the Trullo Sovrano was built with mortar. It's not one of those dismantlable buildings from the fiscal legend. It was built to last.

The complex was called "Corte Papa Cataldo" — and the surrounding trulli were the homes of the priest's workers. A miniature feudal court, all in stone.

Today it's a museum. Worth going in.

---

### CH. 7 — LIVING IN A TRULLO TODAY
*(on foot, walking through the district)*

You've been told that trulli were built to avoid taxes. You've been told they're cool in summer. You've been told they're beautiful.

What nobody tells you is how hard it is to actually live in one today.

A traditional trullo has no roof windows. Light comes in from the sides only. Winter damp is real. The walls are thick but not insulated in any modern sense. You can wire them for electricity; plumbing is complicated; heating is a challenge.

People who've bought a trullo to turn into a B&B have spent serious money to make it liveable. Underfloor heating, treated walls, bathrooms carved out where none existed. The result is often stunning. But it's no longer a trullo as my grandfather lived in one.

Those who still live in one the old way — and there are still some, mostly in Rione Aia Piccola — do it with all the inconvenience and all the pride it takes.

There are no easy stone houses. There are people who love them enough to stay.

---

### CH. 8 — THE CHURCH OF SANT'ANTONIO
*(on foot, in front of the Church of Sant'Antonio)*

Look at this church.

It's built in the shape of a trullo. The only church in the world built this way. The main cone is twenty-one and a half metres tall.

The first stone was laid on January 1st, 1926. The church was inaugurated on June 13th, 1927 — the feast day of Saint Anthony of Padua. Fourteen months of work.

It was the idea of Don Antonio Lippolis, a local priest. The reason was simple: the people of Rione Monti were poor, and to attend Mass they had to walk a long way to the main church. Don Antonio wanted a church in the district.

The problem was the heritage rules. Since 1910, Rione Monti had been a National Monument, and the regulations banned modern-style construction that would "mar the originality" of the district. So Don Antonio made the most logical choice: build like a trullo, like everything else.

The master builders were all local men from Alberobello. We know their names: Giuseppe Notarnicola, Michelangelo La Sorte, Tommaso Curri, Martino De Leonardis. The head builders were the Romano brothers, Francesco and Cosimo, and Tommaso Marzano.

A church built by the people who lived around it, for the people who lived around it. In a form no one had ever used for a church before. In 1927.

That's not nothing.

### CH. 9 — CLOSING: MICHELE SAYS GOODBYE
*(on foot or getting back in the car)*

So.

You've seen the trulli. You've heard the story of the Acquaviva family and the tax law. You've seen the symbols no one can fully explain. You've seen the tourist district and the one where people still actually live.

What you take home isn't a photo — you already have those. What you take home is this: this place isn't a stage set. It's a practical answer to a practical problem — how to build a home when all you have is stone, no money, and a landlord who doesn't want you to exist.

If you want to eat the way people eat here — not the tourist version — look for a trattoria in Rione Aia Piccola, away from Rione Monti. Orecchiette with tomato sauce, lamb, lampascioni — those are small wild onions, bitter and wonderful. Avoid any restaurant with a menu translated into five languages hanging outside the door.

And if you still have a couple of hours, get back in the car and drive to Locorotondo. Fifteen minutes from here. A different world — white, quiet, with the best wine in the valley.

I'll see you there.
