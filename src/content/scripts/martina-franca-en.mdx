## AUDIO SCRIPT — MARTINA FRANCA GUIDE (EN)
*Voice: direct descendant of the Caracciolo family, anonymous by choice*
*Format: mixed car + walking · 25–30 minutes*
*Note: oral family legends are presented explicitly as such — undocumented family traditions*

---

### CH. 1 — INTRO: A DESCENDANT SPEAKS
*(in the car, approaching Martina Franca)*

I'd rather not give my name.

It's not modesty. It's that certain things, when you say them with a name attached, become statements. And I don't want to make statements — I want to tell stories. There's a difference.

What I know about this city I know because I grew up here, and before me my father grew up here, and his father before him, and others before that. A family that held a place for three hundred years leaves traces — in documents, yes, but above all in the stories that pass from mouth to mouth on summer evenings, in dialect, among people who never thought to write them down.

Some of these stories you'll find in history books. Others you won't. The ones you won't find are the ones I'm going to tell you.

Martina Franca. Come in slowly.

---

### CH. 2 — PETRACONE V: THE DUKE WHO WANTED TO IMPRESS
*(in the car or on arrival)*

My family arrived here in 1507, when Ferdinand the Catholic invested my ancestor with the fief of Martina. Three hundred years of a dukedom, thirteen dukes in all.

But the real protagonist is Petracone V. Eighth duke, in power from 1655 to 1704. He was the first in the family to move here permanently — before him, the dukes administered the fief from a distance, from Naples. Petracone decided he wanted to live here. And since he was living here, he wanted people to know who he was.

In 1668 he began construction of the Palazzo Ducale. Three hundred rooms. An internal theatre. Several chapels. Sixty thousand ducats — an enormous sum for the time. The design was by a Bergamo architect, Giovanni Andrea Carducci, and it was approved by Gian Lorenzo Bernini, who was then superintendent of the Kingdom of Naples.

In the family, it's said that Bernini approved the design without ever coming here. He sent a letter with his observations. My grandfather maintained that letter still existed somewhere among the family papers — but nobody ever found it. Maybe it was a story to make a good impression. Maybe not.

What is certain is that the palace is there, it's imposing, and Bernini put his name on it.

---

### CH. 3 — THE PALACE: THREE HUNDRED ROOMS AND A THEATRE
*(on foot, in front of the Palazzo Ducale)*

What you see now is only a part of the original palace.

Three hundred rooms. When people mention this, they say it like an impressive number, and it is. But what nobody ever says is that most of those rooms weren't state rooms — they were service rooms, storehouses, servants' quarters, stables, kitchens. A seventeenth-century ducal palace was a machine, not just a monument.

The internal theatre is what I miss most. It no longer exists — demolished during the conversion into the Town Hall in 1928. In the family it's told that private performances were held there, that Petracone V would invite theatre companies from Naples. It's also said that one evening, during one of these performances, an actor fell off the stage and broke his leg. The duke, instead of showing concern, laughed. After that, the company never came back.

I don't know if it's true. It's what was told.

Go into the courtyard if you can. That's the heart of the palace — and for fifty years now, every summer, that courtyard becomes an opera stage. But we'll get to that.

---

### CH. 4 — THE BLACKSMITH'S REVOLT
*(on foot, walking through the historic centre)*

In 1646 — twenty years before Petracone V built the palace — there was a revolt.

Not a war, not an army. A blacksmith. They called him "Capo di Ferro" — Iron Head — because he worked iron, or because his head was as hard as iron, depending on who's telling the story.

He led a group of Martina's townspeople against the Caracciolo. The details are unclear even in the historical record — what is known is that it happened, that it was suppressed, and that Capo di Ferro paid for it.

In the family, the story is told differently. It's said that Capo di Ferro wasn't just a blacksmith — that he had connections to certain circles in Naples that wanted to destabilise the dukedom. It's said that my ancestor at the time knew exactly who was behind it, but couldn't say, because they were names too powerful to name.

True or not, the story says something real: three hundred years of power don't pass without someone pushing back. And those who push back end up in stories, not in documents.

---

### CH. 5 — THE FRESCOES AND THE SECRET OF THE WALLED ROOM
*(on foot, in front of the entrance to the Piano Nobile)*

You can go in and visit the rooms of the Piano Nobile — the Arcadia Room, the Mythology Room, the Bible Room. The frescoes are by Domenico Carella, 1776. They were commissioned by Francesco III, son of Petracone V.

Carella was good. These rooms are among the finest in eighteenth-century Puglia.

But there's something you won't see.

In the family, there has always been talk of a walled-up room. Not a romantic legend — something concrete. My grandfather mentioned it as if it were obvious: "the room they closed when the French came." The French, in 1806, with the abolition of feudalism.

What was inside? I don't know. There are at least three versions in the family: documents the Caracciolo didn't want to hand over, silver and valuables, or simply a room that became inaccessible during building work and was never reopened.

If it still exists, it's somewhere inside what is now the Town Hall. As far as I know, nobody has ever seriously looked for it.

---

### CH. 6 — 1806: WHEN IT ALL ENDED
*(on foot, walking through the centre)*

On August 2nd, 1806, Joseph Bonaparte — Napoleon's brother, who ruled the Kingdom of Naples — signed the decree abolishing feudalism.

From that day on, the Caracciolo were no longer dukes of anything. The title remained as a name, a noble predicate, but the power was gone.

The male line died out shortly after — Petracone VIII died in 1827, aged twenty-three. Young, no male heirs. The title passed to his sister Argentina, who married a de Sangro. They became the Caracciolo-De Sangro.

And the palace? What nobody ever says is how fast the fall was. After 1806, the rooms began to be rented to private tenants. Then sold. Some converted into storehouses, into stables. A three-hundred-room palace becoming a tenement, floor by floor.

In 1928 the Municipality of Martina Franca bought the main section. It became the Town Hall you see today.

In the family this period isn't talked about much. Not out of shame — out of something that feels more like grief. Three centuries of history in one place, and then in less than a hundred years it was all dismantled.

That's how power ends. Not in battle. Slowly, room by room.

---

### CH. 7 — THE FESTIVAL: OPERA IN THE FAMILY COURTYARD
*(on foot, in the courtyard of the Palazzo Ducale)*

In 1975 someone had the idea of putting on an opera festival in the palace courtyard.

When I found out — my father told me, he was still a boy — the first reaction was strange. The family courtyard turned into a stage. Chairs set up where carriages used to stand.

Then I understood it was the most fitting thing that could have happened to that place.

The Festival della Valle d'Itria has been running ever since. Every summer, July and August, rare operas — the ones the big theatres don't stage because they're "too difficult" or "too forgotten." Monteverdi, Handel, the Neapolitan bel canto of the eighteenth century. It has won the Premio Abbiati — Italy's top music criticism award — seven times. In 2024 it celebrated fifty years.

In the family it's said that Petracone V would have approved. He built a theatre inside the palace to bring music from Naples — now music comes from across Europe, and sits in his courtyard.

That's not a bad end for a place that saw three hundred years of history.

---

### CH. 8 — THE CAPOCOLLO: OAK SMOKE AND VINCOTTO
*(in the alleys of the old town, or at the market)*

One practical thing before I say goodbye.

The Capocollo di Martina Franca. It's been documented since the eighteenth century, in the Kingdom of Naples. The Caracciolo knew it well — the fragno oak forests around the city were partly ducal property, and fragno wood is what you use for the smoking.

How it's made: the neck of the pig, rubbed with salt, pepper and local aromatic herbs. Then marinated in vincotto — cooked grape must, reduced down to a thick, slightly sweet syrup. Then smoked with fragno oak and almond shells. Then at least one hundred days of ageing.

It's not DOP — the process is underway. But it carries the Slow Food Presidio mark, which counts for something.

If you find a real one, from a Martina producer, buy it. It's one of the few flavours that hasn't changed since my ancestor ate it at the Piano Nobile of the palace.

---

### CH. 9 — CLOSING: A DISCREET FAREWELL
*(on foot or getting back in the car)*

I'll leave it there.

I've told you what gets told in my family. Some of it you'll find written in history books. The rest — the letter from Bernini, the walled room, the actor who fell from the stage — you won't find anywhere, because it was never written down. It's what remains when the documents end and the family memory continues.

You can believe it or not. I grew up inside it.

Martina Franca is worth more than one visit. Come back in July, when the palace courtyard fills with music. Or in December, when the city is empty and you can walk the alleys without meeting anyone.

Those are the moments when you can still feel something of what this place once was.

Safe travels.
